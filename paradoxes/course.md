# Twelve Paradoxes That Broke the Rules
slug: paradoxes
#paradoxes #logic #mathematics #philosophy #infinity #decision_theory
level: standard: curious adults of better-than-average ability with no logic or maths background beyond school; define a term once, in passing
language: English
notation: Each lecture is one long piece (about 36–44 spoken lines, 4½–6 minutes) drawn on graph paper, in the style of the Gödel and Arrow drawcasts. Most lectures are a two-voice conversation: a = the explainer (male, knows the mathematics, draws), b = the co-host (female, dry understated wit, takes the naive side, objects, guesses, sums up). Lectures 1, 3, 5 and 10 have a single narrator (male, the same explainer). Each lecture states its paradox plainly, makes the naive answer feel right, shows exactly where it breaks with one worked example on the canvas, then says what the paradox changed. Sets in curly braces {…}; infinity as ∞; probabilities as fractions or percentages, never both on one line.
example: The thread: every paradox is an argument where each step looks innocent and the conclusion is impossible — so one step must be wrong, and finding which one changed a field. Lectures 1–3 are about infinity, 4–7 about self-reference (Russell, Berry, Gödel, Turing), 8–12 about reasoning and choice (ending with Arrow on voting). A lecture may refer back to an earlier one in a phrase (Cantor's diagonal returns in Russell and in the halting problem; the liar returns in Berry), never a recap.

Twelve arguments where every step looks fine and the conclusion is impossible — from Zeno's tortoise to Arrow's voting theorem — and what finding the broken step taught mathematics, logic and decision-making.
