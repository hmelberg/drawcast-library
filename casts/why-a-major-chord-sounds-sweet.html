Why a major chord sounds sweet

Why C, E and G sound sweet together while neighbouring keys grate: their vibrations fall back in step as four to five to six.

Why do C, E and G sound so sweet together, when two neighbouring keys grate? The answer is in how fast each note vibrates.

Middle C: its string swings back and forth about 262 times a second.

Slow that right down and draw a sliver of it: four swings of C.

E swings faster, about 330 times a second.

So in the time C makes four swings, E makes five.

G is faster still, about 392 a second.

Six swings in the same stretch.

Four, five, six: at the end of the stretch all three begin a swing together, and the pattern repeats exactly, over and over.

The ear hears waves that keep falling back in step as one smooth, settled sound.

Now C with its neighbour, C sharp. Hear how they rub.

In C's four swings, C sharp makes about four and a quarter. The two slip out of step and take a long time to meet again.

Back to E, and back in step.

So the ear likes small whole numbers. C, E and G vibrate almost exactly as four to five to six.

The Pythagoreans found such ratios on strings 2,500 years ago: a string two thirds as long sounds a fifth higher, like C up to G.

Which pair sounds smoothest together?

C and G: two swings against three, so their waves fall back in step almost at once.
