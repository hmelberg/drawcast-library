The Ambassadors: what is on the floor?

Holbein's Ambassadors shows off everything two young men knew and owned, then hides death where only a step to the side reveals it.

This is The Ambassadors, painted by Hans Holbein in London in 1533.

And our question is: what is that strange grey smear across the floor doing in it?

At first sight, it's a show-off portrait: two rich, clever young men, life size.

On the left stands Jean de Dinteville, the French king's ambassador to England.

Holbein even wrote his age on the sheath of his dagger: twenty-nine.

His friend Georges de Selve, a young bishop, leans on a book that gives his own age: twenty-five.

Between them stand two shelves, and the whole picture is built in three layers.

First the heavens, then the earth, and last of all, the floor.

The top shelf is the heavens: a globe of the stars, and instruments for telling the time by the sun.

The lower shelf comes down to earth, with a globe on which Dinteville's own home, Polisy, is marked.

Next to it lies a lute, and one of its strings has snapped.

Beside the lute lies a Lutheran hymn book — a striking thing to put next to a Catholic bishop, just as Henry the Eighth was breaking with Rome.

A broken string beside the songs of a church splitting in two: many read it as a picture of discord, though Holbein left no note to say so.

So far, everything says learning, wealth and power — and then there is the floor.

Something long and grey lies across it, at a slant.

What is the long grey shape on the floor? Pick one.

A skull — stretched so far that, from the front, it is only a smear.

Stand close to the painting's right-hand edge and look along it, and the smear snaps into a skull.

The trick is called anamorphosis: a picture stretched so it only looks right from one spot.

And it isn't the only skull: Dinteville wears a tiny one as a badge on his hat.

One more thing hides here, half behind the green curtain. Where is the hidden cross? Tap it.

Christ on the cross, squeezed into the corner: the one thing in the room that looks past death.

So the painting is built in layers: what the men know and own, a world falling out of tune, death on the floor, and, barely in view, the promise of the cross.

Look straight at the ambassadors and you see their success; step to the side, and the same picture tells you that none of it lasts.

A photographer wants a portrait of a tech billionaire that quietly says success doesn't last. Which choice follows Holbein's method?

A hidden detail: like the skull, the message waits until the viewer moves.

So next time you stand in front of a painting, take a step to the side. Holbein expected you to.
