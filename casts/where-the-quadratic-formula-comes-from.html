Where the quadratic formula comes from

How completing the square, drawn as an actual square, turns x² + 6x = 16 into the quadratic formula for every equation.

Where does the quadratic formula come from? From a picture. Take this equation: x squared plus six x makes sixteen.

With x in two places, you can't simply undo the steps one by one.

So draw it. x squared is a square with side x.

Six x is two strips, each three by x, laid along two of its sides. Together they cover sixteen.

Now the shape is almost a square — one corner is missing, three by three. Fill it in: that is nine more.

Add nine to both sides, and sixteen becomes twenty-five.

And the left side is now a whole square, with side x plus three.

A square of area twenty-five has a side of five — x appears only once now.

Five — or minus five, since minus five squared is twenty-five as well.

So x is two, or minus eight. Both work.

That move is completing the square, and it is literally a square. Al-Khwarizmi drew this picture in Baghdad around 820, in the book whose title gave us the word algebra.

Now do the same with letters instead of numbers — any a, b and c.

Divide by a first. Then each strip is b over two a, times x…

…and the missing corner is b over two a, squared.

Out comes the quadratic formula. It is this picture, done once for every quadratic there is.

The plus-or-minus is the five and the minus five.

And the number under the root plays the twenty-five: if it is negative, no square has that area, and there is no real solution.

For x² + 2x + 5 = 0, b² − 4ac is 4 − 20 = −16. How many real solutions?

None: the square would need an area of minus sixteen, and no real number squares to a negative.
