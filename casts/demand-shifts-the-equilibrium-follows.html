How the price finds its way

How a shortage pushes a market to its new price after demand jumps, and why slow-growing crops can circle that price forever.

When demand shifts, does a market really move to the new equilibrium, and how? Take strawberries at one market: crates a day, and the price of a crate.

Growers bring more crates, the better the price.

Buyers take fewer, the dearer they are.

They meet at fifty dollars and thirty crates, and that is where the market price sits.

Then a heat wave: at every price, buyers want twenty more crates, and demand slides right.

Nobody can see the new crossing. What everyone sees is this: at fifty dollars, buyers want fifty crates and growers bring thirty.

Stalls sell out by noon, and buyers ask for more. The shortage is the signal.

So sellers ask more. Each step up brings more crates in and talks some buyers out of it, until the gap is gone.

It closes at the new crossing: sixty dollars, forty crates. Nobody had to find it; the shortage led there.

But that assumes growers can answer at once. Strawberries are planted a season ahead, on last season's price.

Fifty dollars brings only thirty crates, so buyers bid them up to seventy. Seventy brings fifty crates, a glut, and the price falls back to fifty.

And round again. With these curves the price never settles: it jumps between fifty and seventy, circling the sixty it never reaches.

Arthur Hanau found such swings in German pig prices in 1928. Economists still call it the hog cycle.

This wedge is what buyers and growers gain from trading. It is largest at the crossing, so every lap of the cycle wastes some of it.

So the equilibrium does pull the price, through shortage and glut. Whether the price arrives depends on how fast supply can answer.

What if growers reacted to price less strongly than buyers?

The swings die out: each crop overshoots less than the last, so the price spirals in to the crossing.
