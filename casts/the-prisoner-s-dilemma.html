Why two rational suspects both confess

Why two suspects who each do what is best for them both end up worse off, and how meeting again and again lets cooperation pay.

Why do two people end up worse off when each does what's best for them? The classic case: two suspects, questioned in separate rooms.

If both stay silent, the police can prove only a small crime: one year each. The numbers are years in prison, A's first.

If one confesses and the other stays silent, the confessor walks free and the other gets ten years.

If both confess, five years each.

Take A's view. If B stays silent, confessing means walking free instead of one year.

If B confesses, confessing means five years instead of ten. Whatever B does, A does better by confessing.

B reasons the same way: whatever A does, B does better by confessing too.

So both confess and get five years — the Nash equilibrium — though silence would have cost them one year each.

Why not promise each other silence? Alone in the room, breaking the promise still pays. Nothing in the table has changed.

One way out: meet again and again, as firms, neighbours and countries do.

Round one: both stay silent.

Say B is tempted in round two and confesses. A plays tit for tat: start silent, then copy what the other did last round.

So in round three A confesses. Betray once, and you are punished the very next round.

Go back to silence, and so does the other. Today's betrayal costs tomorrow's cooperation.

In Robert Axelrod's 1980 tournaments of computer strategies, this simplest one, sent in by Anatol Rapoport, won.

The suspects meet once, but talk first and promise to stay silent. What happens?

Each still does better by confessing: a promise changes no number in the table, and in one meeting there is no next round to punish a betrayal.
