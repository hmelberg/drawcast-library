Why did Darwin wait twenty years?

Darwin had natural selection by 1838 but published in 1859: what he did with those twenty years, and the letter that ended them

Why did Charles Darwin wait twenty years to publish natural selection? He had it worked out by 1838, and the book came out in 1859.

Here are those years to scale. First the Beagle's voyage round the world, 1831 to 1836: finches, fossils, and growing doubts that species are fixed.

Home in 1837, he sketched a branching tree in a notebook under the words 'I think'. A year later, reading Malthus, he had the mechanism.

The book, On the Origin of Species, came out in November 1859.

Twenty-one years apart: most of the line. The usual story is that he feared the scandal, and hid the idea in a drawer.

But the drawer was full. By 1844 he had written the theory out as a long essay, with a letter asking his wife Emma to publish it if he died.

Then eight years on barnacles, 1846 to 1854: the expert standing, and the habit of patient evidence, that a heretical claim would need.

In June 1858, deep in a huge book on species, he opened a letter from Alfred Russel Wallace in the Malay Archipelago. It described the same idea.

Within two weeks both men's papers were read together in London, and the Origin, which Darwin called a mere abstract, followed the next year.

So the idea was finished early. What filled the gap was evidence, and what ended it was a rival.

Historians still argue over his motives, and some doubt it was a delay at all: for a naturalist then, twenty years of evidence was simply thoroughness.

How much of his theory had Darwin written down by 1844?

A long essay, with instructions to publish it if he died: the gap was not an empty drawer but twenty years of gathering evidence.
