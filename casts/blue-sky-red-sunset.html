Why the sky is blue and sunsets red

How one effect, air scattering blue light far more than red, paints the daytime sky blue and turns the setting sun orange and red.

Why is the sky blue, and why do sunsets turn red?

Sunlight arrives white: every colour of the rainbow mixed together.

On its way down it hits air molecules, far smaller than a wavelength of light, and each knocks a little light aside.

How much depends steeply on colour: scattering goes as one over the wavelength to the fourth power.

Blue, at about 450 nanometres, is scattered almost six times more than red at 700.

So wherever you look away from the sun, blue light is arriving from the side, scattered toward you.

That scattered blue is the sky.

Why not violet, which scatters even more?

The sun sends less violet, and our eyes are far less sensitive to it, so the mix looks blue.

Now stand where the sun is setting: its light must cross far more air to reach you.

Near the horizon the path holds about thirty-eight times as much air as straight up, far more than this drawing can show.

Along that long path, blue is scattered out of the beam again and again.

What reaches your eye is what is left: orange and red.

So the blue sky and the red sunset are one effect: the blue knocked out of the beam, and the light that survives.

The Moon has no air. What colour is its sky in the daytime?

Black: with no air there is nothing to scatter sunlight toward you, so you see only the sun itself.
