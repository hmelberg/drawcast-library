Euler's identity, as half a turn

Why e to the i pi equals minus one: multiplying by i is a quarter turn, so e to the i pi is half a turn around the unit circle.

Why does e to the i pi equal minus one? It sounds like nonsense, but it is a picture: half a turn around a circle.

It is often called the most beautiful equation in mathematics. To see why it is true, start with the number one.

Multiplying by i turns a number a quarter turn: one becomes i, straight up.

Turn again and you reach minus one — which is all that i squared equals minus one says.

Between those quarter turns lie all the other angles, on a circle of radius one.

So e to the i theta means: turn from one by an angle theta.

Turn a little, and the point lands cosine theta across and sine theta up.

That is Euler's formula: e to the i theta is cosine theta plus i sine theta.

Now turn by pi radians — a hundred and eighty degrees, half a turn.

Half a turn from one lands exactly on minus one.

So e to the i pi is minus one: not a coincidence of numbers, but a half turn written as a power.

Move the one across, and e, i, pi, one and zero share a single line.

At fourteen, Richard Feynman wrote in his notebook that this was the most remarkable formula in mathematics.

Where does e to the 2iπ land?

At 1: two pi is a full turn, which brings you back to where you started.
