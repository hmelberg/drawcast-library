Why queues explode near 100% busy

Why does a queue explode when a server gets close to 100% busy?

Take one cashier: customers arrive at random, one every two minutes on average, and each takes about a minute.

So she is busy half the time. Let's run an eight-hour day.

Customers come in clumps, a short line forms now and then, and it soon clears.

Over the day, the average wait is under a minute.

Now let more customers come, so that she is busy 80% of the time.

The same day, re-run: sixty percent more customers, and five times the wait.

Queueing theory predicts this curve: the wait grows like busy time divided by idle time.

At 95% busy, idle time is a twentieth, and the predicted wait is nineteen minutes.

This day came out at ten: near full, one day's average swings wildly around the prediction.

Why? Customers arrive in random bursts, and a line only shrinks while she serves faster than they come.

At half busy she has spare time to catch up; at 95% she has almost none, so every burst lingers.

If customers came exactly on schedule and each took exactly a minute, nobody would wait at all: randomness makes the queue.

That is why hospital planners aim for beds about 85% full, not 100%.

Near full, waits don't grow in step with busyness; they blow up, because no slack is left to absorb the randomness.

A server goes from 90% to 95% busy. Roughly what happens to the average wait?

It roughly doubles: busy over idle goes from 9 to 19, because idle time halves.
