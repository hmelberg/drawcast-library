The Tower of Hanoi: why 2ⁿ − 1 moves

Why moving a tower of n disks takes exactly 2ⁿ − 1 moves: the doubling at the heart of recursion, then solve the puzzle yourself.

The Tower of Hanoi asks how few moves shift a whole tower to the right peg. The rules: one disk at a time, never a larger on a smaller.

Take three disks. Before the biggest can move anywhere, what has to be true?

Both disks above it must be out of the way, stacked on the middle peg. That is a two-disk tower, moved: three moves.

Then the big disk crosses, in one move.

And the two-disk tower moves again, onto it: three more. Three, one, three: seven.

The same holds for any size: to move n disks, move the tower above the biggest twice, plus one move for the biggest.

That twice is the whole story. Each extra disk doubles the work, and adds one.

One, three, seven, fifteen, thirty-one: always one less than a power of two. A guess of two more per disk would say nine for four disks; it takes fifteen.

Édouard Lucas sold the puzzle in 1883 with a legend of monks moving sixty-four disks. At a move a second, that takes about 585 billion years.

Back to the start. Now it's your tower.

Move the tower to the right peg. Can you do it in seven?

Solved. Seven is the fewest: the small pair aside in three, the big disk over, the pair back on top in three.

Five disks take 31 moves. How many does six take?

63: the five-disk tower moves twice, 31 moves each, plus one move for the biggest disk.
