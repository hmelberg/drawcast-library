Why screening can look better than it is

How screening can add years of 'survival' without adding a single day of life, and why only fewer deaths prove it works.

Why can screening look better than it is? Survival is counted from the day of diagnosis, and screening moves that day. One patient's timeline shows how.

First, life without screening. A cancer starts at year one, silently.

By year two and a half a test could find it, but nobody is testing.

Symptoms appear at year five and a half, and that is when it is diagnosed.

The patient dies at year eight and a half. Survival, counted from diagnosis: three years.

Now the same patient and the same cancer, but screened.

A test at year three finds it, two and a half years before any symptom.

Suppose treating it early changes nothing. Death still comes at eight and a half, and the dashed line shows the very same moment.

Now survival is five and a half years. This patient counts as a five-year survivor, and died on the same day.

The gain is only lead time: the stopwatch started earlier, and the finish line never moved.

So the honest test is deaths, not survival. In a trial, do fewer screened people die of the cancer than unscreened people?

Real trials show it. The Mayo Lung Project, begun in the 1970s, screened smokers with chest X-rays. Lung-cancer survival rose, yet lung-cancer deaths did not fall.

Screening can save lives, but only when finding it early moves this cross. Only a count of deaths can show that it did.

A new screening test raises five-year survival from 40% to 70%. What does that prove?

Nothing yet: an earlier diagnosis lengthens survival even when death doesn't move. Only fewer deaths in a trial shows that screening saves lives.
