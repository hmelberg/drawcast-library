Why 1900 had no leap day

Why February gets a 29th day every four years, and why skipping it in 1900 but not in 2000 keeps the calendar on time.

Most of us learned that every fourth year is a leap year.

Yet 1900 had no twenty-ninth of February, and 2000 did, so what is the rule really for?

It starts with the sun: from one spring to the next takes 365 days, plus almost six hours.

A calendar of exactly 365 days drops those hours, so each year spring arrives about six hours later by the date.

After four years the leftovers come to nearly a whole day, and so Julius Caesar's calendar added one: a leap day every fourth year.

But four lots of five hours forty-nine make twenty-three hours sixteen, not twenty-four.

So every leap day adds about three quarters of an hour too much, which is eleven minutes for every year.

Eleven minutes is nothing in a lifetime, but by 1582 the calendar had run ten days ahead of where the Church had fixed the spring equinox in 325, the day Easter is counted from: it now fell on the eleventh of March.

So Pope Gregory the Thirteenth cut the ten days out: Thursday the fourth of October 1582 was followed by Friday the fifteenth.

That reset the calendar, but with the old rule it would simply drift away again: four more days by 2100.

The fix is to drop a leap day now and then.

A century of overshoots comes to about three quarters of a day, so the century years lose their leap day: 1700, 1800 and 1900.

But three quarters is not a whole day, so skipping every century would cut one day too many in four hundred years.

And so the centuries divisible by four hundred keep their leap day, which is why 2000 had its twenty-ninth of February.

That makes 97 leap days in every four hundred years, and the error falls from eleven minutes a year to under half a minute.

Not everyone made the switch: some Orthodox churches still keep the Julian calendar, so for them 1900 was a leap year.

So their gap grew to thirteen days, and their Christmas, the twenty-fifth of December, now falls on our seventh of January.

A leap year is a correction, 1900 was a correction of the correction, and 2000 corrected that one in turn.
