What p < 0.05 really means

A blood-pressure trial on one curve: what a p-value measures, the misreading that flips it, and why 0.05 is only a convention.

What does p below 0.05 actually tell you? It is research's most common verdict, and its most misread. This curve shows what it does and doesn't say.

A blood-pressure drug is tested against placebo. If the drug did nothing, the gap between the groups would still wobble around zero, by chance.

With this trial's size, the wobble has a standard error of 2 millimetres. Most results land within two of these steps.

Before the trial, mark the five percent most extreme results a useless drug could produce: beyond 1.96 steps, 2.5 percent in each tail.

The trial finds blood pressure 5 millimetres lower on the drug: 2.5 standard errors from zero, inside the shaded region.

The p-value is the area beyond the result itself: if the drug did nothing, results this extreme would come up 1.2 percent of the time.

Both tails count, because a drug raising pressure by 5 would have been just as surprising. That is what two-sided means.

So the p-value is the chance of data this extreme, given no effect.

The common misreading flips it: a 1.2 percent chance that the drug does nothing, given the data.

That is a different number. It depends on how likely the drug was to work before the trial, which the p-value never uses.

Nor does it measure size: a trial of many thousands could make a trivial half-millimetre drop just as significant.

The 0.05 line itself is a convention.

Ronald Fisher suggested it in 1925 as a convenient limit for judging a result, not a law.

A trial reports p = 0.012. Which statement is right?

If the drug did nothing, results this extreme would occur 1.2 percent of the time. The p-value assumes no effect; it cannot give the chance of no effect, or its size.
