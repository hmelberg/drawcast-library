Simpson's paradox: win both, lose overall

How a drug can win among mild cases and among severe cases yet lose overall — and why randomising the mix makes the paradox vanish.

Can a drug win for mild cases and for severe cases, yet lose overall? It can, and one picture shows where the reversal comes from.

Drug A cures ninety percent of mild cases, sixty percent of severe ones. Overall, it depends on the mix: all mild at the left, all severe at the right.

Drug B cures eighty-five and fifty-five. Its line runs five points below A's, whatever the mix.

So among mild cases, A wins by five.

Among severe cases, A wins by five again.

A drug's overall rate is a dot on its own line, at the share of severe patients it was given.

With the same mix, A's dot sits right above B's.

But suppose doctors saved drug A for the hardest cases: eighty percent of its patients were severe, and only twenty percent of B's.

Pooled, A cures sixty-six percent and B seventy-nine. B wins by thirteen, though it lost both comparisons.

Neither line moved. The dots slid along them: A's pooled rate is mostly a severe-case rate, B's mostly a mild-case rate.

The honest comparison puts both dots on one vertical line: the same mix of patients.

Give both drugs half mild and half severe, and A leads by five, as it did in each group.

A randomised trial does this for you: chance, not a doctor, picks who gets which drug, so both get the same mix on average.

The 1948 British trial of streptomycin for tuberculosis is often called the first to assign patients by random numbers.

In a randomised trial, where would the two dots sit?

On one vertical line: chance gives both drugs the same mix of patients on average, so only the gap between the lines shows.
