How a magnifying glass really works

Trace two light rays through a lens to find the bigger letter, and see that what really magnifies is letting your eye get close.

How does a magnifying glass make a letter look bigger, and where is the bigger letter? Here is the lens, edge-on.

Its focal length is ten centimetres: parallel light meets at F, ten centimetres out on either side.

Hold a small letter 7.5 centimetres away, just inside the focal length.

A ray leaving the top of the letter parallel to the axis is bent through the far F.

A ray through the centre of the lens goes straight on.

Past the lens these two rays spread apart. They never meet, so there is no picture on this side for a screen to catch.

But your eye can't know the rays were bent. It traces them straight back, to where they seem to come from.

They seem to come from here: an upright letter thirty centimetres behind the glass, four times as tall. A virtual image.

Four times as tall, but four times as far away: it fills the same angle as the letter itself, seen from 7.5 centimetres.

That is the trick. Your bare eye can't focus closer than about twenty-five centimetres, where the letter looks small.

The lens moves the image out past that limit, so you see the letter sharp as if from 7.5 centimetres: over three times bigger.

Slide the letter out to 8.5 centimetres: the image is now nearly seven times the letter's height, but 57 centimetres away.

Height over distance hardly changes. What magnifies is getting close; the lens only makes close focusable.

The near point creeps outward with age, which is why people past about forty start holding menus at arm's length.

A stronger magnifier has a shorter focal length. Why does it magnify more?

It lets you hold the object closer: a shorter focal length puts the letter nearer your eye while its image stays far enough away to focus.
