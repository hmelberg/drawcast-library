Who pays a ticket tax?

Why a $10 ticket tax is split by who can walk away more easily, not by who hands the money to the government.

When the government taxes every concert ticket by ten dollars, who really pays it?

Start with the ticket market before any tax: fans on one side…

…sellers on the other.

They meet at fifty dollars a ticket.

Now the law says the seller must send ten dollars per ticket to the government, so the whole supply curve lifts by ten.

The seller would love to just add ten dollars to the price.

But at sixty dollars some fans stay home, so the price settles in between.

Fans now pay fifty-five, and sellers keep forty-five.

The ten-dollar gap between those two prices is the government's cut, split five and five.

That even split came from assuming both sides can back out equally easily.

A concert is different: the hall has the same number of seats tonight, whatever the price.

So the supply curve is about to stand up straight.

Now the fans' price barely moves above fifty.

And the sellers' take drops to just over forty: they carry almost the whole tax.

Empty seats earn nothing, so the promoter cannot refuse the lower price the way a fan can refuse a higher one.

Whoever has fewer ways out pays more of the tax, no matter who writes the cheque to the government.

With a fixed number of seats, who bears most of the $10 ticket tax?

The sellers: with the seats fixed they cannot walk away, so the price fans pay barely rises and the tax comes out of what sellers keep.
