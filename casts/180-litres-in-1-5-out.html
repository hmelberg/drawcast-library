Why your kidneys filter 180 litres a day only to take nearly all of it back, and how a percent of that sets your water balance.

How much urine do your kidneys make? About one and a half litres a day, but they start from far more. It begins in a glomerulus.

Blood pressure pushes plasma through this knot of capillaries.

Cells and proteins stay in the blood; water, salt, sugar and waste fall into the capsule.

Your two million or so nephrons filter about a hundred and eighty litres a day: all your plasma, some sixty times over.

So the rest of the tubule is about taking it back. The proximal tubule reclaims about two thirds of the water and salt at once.

And normally all the glucose.

But when blood sugar runs too high, this step is swamped and sugar spills into the urine: that is why it is called diabetes mellitus: mellitus means honey-sweet.

Then the loop of Henle dives into the medulla, where the tissue grows saltier the deeper it goes.

Going down, water seeps out into that salt. Coming up, salt is pumped out but water can't follow — that is what keeps the deep tissue salty.

The distal tubule trims the sodium, on orders from the hormone aldosterone.

The collecting duct runs back down past the salty zone. Antidiuretic hormone opens water channels here, and the salt pulls water out.

What's left is urine: about one and a half litres of the hundred and eighty. Over ninety-nine percent came back.

Why filter so much only to take it back? Because anything the tubule doesn't choose to reclaim — waste, toxins, drugs — leaves by default.

It also makes control fine. Keep ninety-nine percent of 180 litres, and you pass 1.8.

Keep ninety-eight, and it doubles to 3.6.

So your water balance is set here, by a percent or two of reabsorption, not by filtering less.

You drink two extra litres of water. What mainly changes?

The collecting duct keeps less water: filtration stays near 180 litres, and with less antidiuretic hormone more of it is let go as urine.
