Thales' theorem

Why any angle drawn in a semicircle is exactly ninety degrees, proved with nothing more than three equal radii.

Why is an angle drawn in a semicircle always exactly ninety degrees? Start with a circle and a diameter from A to B.

Pick a point C on the circle and join it to both ends. Wherever C sits, this angle measures ninety degrees. But why?

The key is the centre, O. Draw one more line, from O to C: it is a radius.

O A and O B are radii too. Three equal lengths, and they split the big triangle into two smaller ones.

Take the left one, A O C. Two of its sides are radii, so it is isosceles.

An isosceles triangle has equal base angles: here sixty-five degrees at A, and the same sixty-five at C. Call it a.

The right one, B O C, is isosceles too: twenty-five degrees at B and twenty-five at C. Call it b.

Now the whole triangle A B C. Its angles are a at A, b at B, and a plus b at C, and they add to 180.

Halve both sides: a plus b is ninety. And a plus b is exactly the angle at C.

So the angle at C is a right angle, forced by nothing more than three equal radii.

Move C anywhere else on the circle and the base angles change, but they always pair up the same way: ninety again.

Off the circle, it fails. From a point inside, the diameter looks wider: 129 degrees here. From outside, narrower: 77.

The story goes that Thales, around 600 BC, sacrificed an ox when he found this: it is among the first theorems anyone is said to have proved.

C moves so that its angle at A becomes 40°. What is the angle at B?

Fifty degrees: the base angles still pair up at C, so the angles at A and B add to ninety wherever C sits.
