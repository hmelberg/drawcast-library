Bayes and the screening test

Why a positive result from a good test can still mean only a one-in-ten chance of disease, worked out with a table and then Bayes' rule.

Suppose a test for a disease comes back positive. How worried should you be? To find out, test a thousand people and sort them two ways.

Say one in a hundred has the disease, so ten of our thousand are sick.

The test is good at finding them. It catches nine of the ten.

It misses one, who goes home reassured.

But it also raises the alarm for nine percent of the healthy, and there are nine hundred and ninety of them. That makes eighty-nine false alarms.

The other nine hundred and one are rightly cleared.

So now read only the top row, everyone who tested positive. Ninety-eight people, and just nine of them are sick. A positive result means about a nine percent chance of disease.

Not ninety. Nine.

Why is it so low? Because the false alarms come from a much bigger group. Eighty-nine of them outnumber the nine true positives almost ten to one.

However good the test is at finding the sick, there are so few of them that the healthy majority swamps them.

The rule that does it is named after the Reverend Thomas Bayes, who worked on it in the seventeen-hundreds. It turns one probability around into the other.

And the key ingredient is the starting chance, how common the disease is before anyone is tested.

Put our numbers in, and the rule gives the same nine percent the table did, without counting heads.

The same test is used in a clinic where half the patients are sick. Is a positive result more or less trustworthy there?

More. With half the patients sick, true positives are common, so a positive result is much more likely to be right.
