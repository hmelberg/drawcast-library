Apollo 11: the landing

Two computer alarms, a boulder field and a fuel count in seconds: how Eagle still landed on the Moon in July 1969.

This is Eagle, standing on the Moon in July 1969, photographed by Neil Armstrong during his moonwalk.

Its last thirteen minutes of flight brought two computer alarms, a field of boulders, and a fuel count down to seconds.

So how did it land at all?

The story starts four days earlier in Florida, when a Saturn V rose from the pad on the morning of 16 July.

On top were Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin, who would land, and Michael Collins, who would wait for them in orbit around the Moon.

Once in orbit around the Moon, on 20 July, Armstrong and Aldrin undocked in Eagle, and Collins took this picture of it from the command ship, Columbia.

From there Eagle’s descent engine lit fifteen kilometres above the Moon, and the computer began to fly them down.

But five minutes in, ten kilometres up, the computer flashed an alarm the crew had never seen: twelve oh two.

In Houston the man on the radio was Charlie Duke, a fellow astronaut, and behind him the guidance team had seconds to decide.

The guidance team knew that a twelve oh two meant the computer was overloaded: a radar switch set the wrong way was feeding it useless work.

But Margaret Hamilton's team at MIT had built its software to drop lesser jobs and keep the vital ones, so it flew on.

And they had been caught out once already: weeks before, a rehearsal flown by two of the men here, Dave Scott and Jim Irwin, had ended in a needless abort over just such an alarm.

So the team knew the codes, and Duke answered at once: we're go on that alarm.

The alarms came back, a twelve oh one at nine hundred metres and another twelve oh two, and each time the answer was go.

But they had come in long, miles past the planned spot, so Armstrong looked out to see where the computer was taking them.

Out of the window it looked bad: the computer was steering them straight toward a crater strewn with boulders.

So at about a hundred and sixty metres he took over by hand and flew on past it, hunting for somewhere smooth.

He was ready for that, because a month before he had practised these last minutes again and again, in a flying trainer like this one.

But all that searching burned fuel, and fifty metres up a low-fuel light came on and started a countdown to bingo.

Bingo was the deadline: from there they would have twenty seconds to land, or they must abort.

So Aldrin called out height and speed, while Duke counted the time down from Houston: sixty seconds.

Then dust began to stream across the ground, hiding how fast they drifted, and Duke called again: thirty seconds.

Moments later, at 20:17 GMT, a probe under a footpad touched the ground: contact light, about twenty-five seconds before bingo.

Then Armstrong radioed home: Houston, Tranquility Base here. The Eagle has landed.

Duke answered that a bunch of guys were about to turn blue: we're breathing again.

In fact they had more than they knew: sloshing fuel had lit the warning early, so the real margin was about half a minute more.

And six and a half hours later Armstrong stepped onto the Moon, and Aldrin followed, filmed here from Eagle's window.

The next day Eagle's top half climbed back to Collins, and three days after that, all three were home.

So how did they land? Not because nothing went wrong, but because every part had room for something to go wrong.

A computer that shed its lesser work, a team that had rehearsed the scare, and a pilot who could fly the last minute by hand, with seconds to spare.
