The causes of the Second World War

How a resented peace, a toothless League and the slump made a second war possible, and why four unanswered gambles brought it in 1939.

On the first of September 1939, German troops crossed into Poland, and barriers like this one came down.

Twenty years earlier, a treaty had ended the Great War. So how did Europe get from that peace to this barrier?

Historians answer in layers: long causes that made a war possible, short ones that made it come, and a trigger that set the day.

The long causes begin with the peace itself. This is the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles in June 1919, where the Germans came to sign a treaty written without them.

Its clauses read like a charge sheet, and the first of them stung most: Article 231 put the blame for the whole war on Germany and her allies.

Here is the treaty as London printed it, signatures in facsimile; Germans called it a dictated peace, a

Diktat.

From that guilt came reparations: in 1921 the bill was set at a hundred and thirty-two billion gold marks.

Germany lost a tenth of its people, and union with Austria was forbidden.

Its army was cut to a hundred thousand men, and the Rhineland, on France's border, was to hold no troops at all; so nearly every party in Germany promised to undo the treaty.

The peace did have a guardian, the League of Nations, meeting here in Geneva; but it had no army of its own, and America never joined.

That weakness showed when Italy invaded Ethiopia: its emperor, Haile Selassie, came to Geneva to plead for help, and got sanctions that did not stop the war.

So two long causes: a peace Germany hated, and no one ready to enforce it.

Then a third blow came from across the Atlantic. In October 1929 the market crashed on Wall Street, crowds gathered outside the exchange, and American banks called home their loans to Germany.

Without those loans German firms failed, and by early 1932 nearly six million Germans were out of work, one figure here for each million.

And a party that had won under three per cent of the vote in 1928 was rising.

Six months later, on the thirtieth of January 1933, Hitler stands among his party's leaders: that day, the President made him chancellor.

That is the third long cause: a slump that gave power to the one man who meant to use the other two.

Those three made a war possible. What made it come were gambles: between 1936 and 1939, Hitler bet four times that no one would stop him.

Four of the players stand behind these cards, at Munich in 1938. Hitler wants the treaty torn up and land in the east, Mussolini an empire; Chamberlain and Daladier want no second war.

The fifth, Stalin, was not in the room, and he trusted none of them.

On a map of central Europe, with today's borders, every gamble starts in Berlin.

The first came in March 1936, when German troops marched into the Rhineland, against the treaty, and France and Britain did nothing.

Two years later, in March 1938, they crossed into Austria, and were cheered in.

In Vienna, crowds packed the Heldenplatz to hear Hitler proclaim the union; the treaty had forbidden it, and again no one moved.

That autumn he demanded the Sudetenland, the German-speaking rim of Czechoslovakia, and threatened war.

Chamberlain flew home from Munich, where Britain and France gave Hitler the Sudetenland, waving a paper Hitler had also signed; that evening he called it peace for our time.

A Dutch newsreel that week shows the papers going out; one front page reads: it is peace.

Six months later he took Prague, land that was not German at all, and Britain and France at last promised to defend Poland.

Back on the board, those four gambles are the short causes, and each one taught Hitler something. The Rhineland taught him that France would not march alone.

Austria taught him that a forbidden union could be cheered in.

Munich, that Britain and France would sign away another country's border to keep the peace.

But Prague taught the others at last that his word was worth nothing, so when he turned to Poland they had promised to fight.

That left the trigger, and in August 1939 it came as a handshake that nobody had expected.

In the Kremlin, on the twenty-third of August, Stalin shook hands with Hitler's foreign minister, Ribbentrop, on a pact that neither would attack the other.

So the man who trusted no one now stood beside Hitler.

And a second paper went with the pact, one that Moscow denied existed until 1989.

This is that secret protocol. Its second point drew a line through Poland along three rivers, the Narew, the Vistula and the San: the west for Hitler, the east for Stalin.

With Russia out of the way, Hitler invaded Poland on the first of September; two days later Britain and France, bound by their promise, declared war.

So, from that peace to that barrier: the treaty left Germany angry, the League left it unchecked, and the slump put Hitler in charge.

But the war came because each gamble that went unanswered made the next one look safe,

until only war was left to answer it, at a barrier like this one.

Which clause put the blame for the war on Germany?

Article 231, the war-guilt clause; the reparations followed from it.

Why could the League not stop Italy in Ethiopia?

It had no army of its own, and its sanctions did not stop the war.

What did France and Britain do when German troops entered the Rhineland in 1936?

They protested and did nothing, and the next gamble looked safer.

Why did the pact with Stalin matter for the attack on Poland?

With Russia out of the way, and Poland shared in secret, Hitler could attack.

What did the secret protocol of August 1939 do?

It drew a line through Poland: the west for Hitler, the east for Stalin.
