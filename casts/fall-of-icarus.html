The Fall of Icarus: where is he?

Bruegel's Fall of Icarus makes the myth's hero the smallest thing in the picture, and nobody in it looks up. Here is why.

This is Landscape with the Fall of Icarus, based on a design by Pieter Bruegel from the late fifteen-fifties.

Bruegel was a Flemish painter, best known for crowded scenes of country people at work.

In the Greek myth, Icarus escapes from Crete on wings his father Daedalus made of feathers and wax.

He flies too close to the sun, the wax melts, and he falls into the sea.

So the title promises a dramatic fall. But where is it? Where is Icarus? Tap him.

That's the hero of the title: two legs by the big ship, already going under.

Meanwhile, the biggest figure in the whole picture is a ploughman.

He comes from Ovid, the Roman poet whose telling of the myth names three people who saw them fly: a ploughman, a shepherd and an angler.

In Ovid, all three look up in astonishment, taking the flyers for gods.

Bruegel keeps all three, but changes what they do. The ploughman keeps his eyes down on his furrow.

The shepherd does look up, but at the empty sky, turned away from the sea.

And the angler, closest of all, watches only his line.

Even the great ship sails on past the splash.

One creature does seem to look: a bird on a branch, often read as a partridge.

In Ovid, Daedalus once tried to kill his gifted nephew, and the goddess Minerva saved the boy by turning him into a partridge.

So some read the bird as that nephew, watching his old attacker lose a son.

One more twist: this canvas in Brussels is probably a copy of a lost original, though the design is Bruegel's.

Make the hero tiny and the ploughman huge, and the myth turns around.

A boy's death becomes a splash at the edge of an ordinary working day.

The tragedy is not only that Icarus falls. It's that nobody looks.

A painter wants to show the cost of a war the way Bruegel shows Icarus. Which picture follows his method?

The family at supper: everyday life in front, the disaster small and far off, and nobody looking.

And you did what nobody in the painting did: you went looking for him.
