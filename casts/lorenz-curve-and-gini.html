Two countries, one Gini

How the Lorenz curve turns into the Gini coefficient, and why two very different countries can share the same Gini.

The Gini coefficient squeezes a country's whole income distribution into one number. To see what it keeps and what it drops, start with a curve.

Line everyone up from poorest to richest, and plot the share of all income held by each share of the people. With equal incomes, that is this diagonal.

A real distribution sags below it. In country A, the poorer half of people get 30 percent of the income, not 50.

Here: half the people, 30 percent of the income.

Everything below the diagonal makes up this triangle, half of the square.

The Gini is the share of that triangle lying between the diagonal and the curve.

For A it is 0.27. Zero would be perfect equality; one, a single person holding everything.

Now country B. Its poorest 40 percent get 25 percent of the income, against 20 percent in A.

But its richest tenth take 24 percent of the income, against 17 in A. The two curves cross.

And B's Gini is also 0.27. What B's curve gains at the bottom it loses at the top, and the areas balance exactly.

So which country is more unequal? When curves cross, it depends on whether you care more about the bottom or the top, and one number has chosen for you.

Max Lorenz drew his curve in 1905, as a graduate student.

Corrado Gini turned the idea into a single number in 1912.

So a Gini is a fair summary only when curves do not cross. Otherwise, quote it with a share at each end.

Two countries both have a Gini of 0.35. What follows?

Nothing definite. Equal areas can come from curves that cross, one kinder to the bottom, the other richer at the top.
