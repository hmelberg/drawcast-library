Price elasticity: how much less do people buy?

How one number measures how strongly buyers react to a price, why substitutes and time change it, and when a fare rise loses money.

Everyone knows that when a price goes up, people buy less. The interesting question is how much less.

To see it, take a made-up small town and the demand for its bus rides.

At sixty kroner a ride, people take thirty rides a day.

Now raise the fare to seventy, and the rides drop to twenty.

That jump, from one point to the other, is what elasticity turns into a single number.

It compares the two changes in percent, how much quantity moved, divided by how much the price moved.

So quantity sits on top, and price underneath.

For our buses, the fare rose about seventeen percent while rides fell by a third, which gives an elasticity of about minus two.

Minus two is a big reaction. But some goods barely react at all.

Insulin is the classic case. Double its price, and diabetics still need the same dose, so the curve stands almost upright.

Such a steep line looks dramatic. Does that make it the elastic one?

No, it is the other way round. Steep means quantity hardly moves, and that is inelastic.

Compare one brand of cola. Raise its price a little, and buyers simply reach for another brand, so its curve lies almost flat.

Now take cola's rivals off the shelf, one by one, and watch its curve tilt up toward insulin's.

Put them back, and it flattens again.

Back in our formula, that is exactly why. A steep curve means a small change on top, and so a small elasticity.

In short, what decides it is whether buyers have somewhere else to go.

Back to the buses, because our minus two hides a snag.

Going up from sixty, the fare rose seventeen percent. But going down from seventy, the same step is only fourteen.

The usual fix is to measure both changes from the midpoint, the average of the two.

For the fares, that is sixty-five.

So ten kroner over sixty-five makes about fifteen percent.

The rides get the same treatment, minus ten over twenty-five, which is minus forty percent.

Dividing one by the other then gives an elasticity of about minus two point six.

And because both changes start from the middle, you get the same number whichever way you go.

Doing this by hand every time gets tedious, so let the computer do it, in Python, running right here in your browser.

We start with a small function for the midpoint percentage change.

Its one idea is the average of the two values, underneath.

Then we give it our two points, fares of sixty and seventy, and thirty and twenty rides.

With those in place, elasticity is just one change over the other, the rides on top and the fares underneath.

Run it, and out comes minus two point six, the same as by hand.

And once the function exists, any new pair of points is a single line.

There is one more thing that bends these numbers, and it is time.

In the first month after a price rise, people mostly grumble and pay.

But give them a year, and they start to adjust, with car pools, a bike, a closer job.

After five years every bar has grown, petrol most of all.

Every good tells the same story, the longer people have, the more they react.

All of this would be a curiosity if it did not decide something that matters to the bus company, its takings.

Its revenue is the fare times the rides, which is this rectangle. Sixty times thirty, eighteen hundred kroner a day.

After the fare rise the rectangle is taller, but much narrower. Seventy times twenty, only fourteen hundred.

It gains the green strip, ten kroner more on each of twenty rides, but loses the red one, ten rides at sixty. So the rise costs the company four hundred kroner a day.

That is what elastic demand means in money. A higher price loses more in rides than it gains per ride.

So revenue falls, even though each ride now costs more.

A ten percent fare rise cuts rides by four percent. What happens to revenue?

It rises. Rides fall less than the fare rose, so demand is inelastic here.
