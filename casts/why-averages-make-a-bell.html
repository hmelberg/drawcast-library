Why averages make a bell

Why the averages of lopsided data, like hospital stays, pile up into a bell — and why doubling the sample doesn't halve the error.

Why do averages come out bell-shaped when the data isn't? Take hospital stays on a short-stay ward, in made-up numbers: most patients leave within two or three days, a few stay a week or more.

In this example the average stay is 2.7 days, pulled to the right of the peak by that long tail.

Now pick four patients at random. Say they stayed one, two, three and six days.

Their average, three days, becomes one dot on the lower line.

Repeat that thousands of times, and the dots pile up into this: averages of four. Already close to a bell, centred on the same 2.7.

The tail can't survive averaging.

An average of seven days needs all four patients to be long stays at once, which almost never happens.

An average near 2.7 can come from countless mixes of short and long stays. More ways in, a taller middle.

The bracket is the typical miss of an average of four: just under a day either way.

Average eight patients instead, twice as many, and the bell narrows: the typical miss falls to about two thirds of a day.

Twice the patients, yet the miss shrank by under a third, not by half: precision grows only with the square root of the sample.

The patients themselves stay as lopsided as ever. The bell isn't in the data; it's in the averaging.

That is the central limit theorem, and it holds for almost any data with a finite spread. George Pólya named it in 1920: central to probability.

Averages of four patients miss by about 0.9 days. How many patients halve that miss?

Sixteen: the miss shrinks with the square root of the sample, so halving it takes four times as many patients.
