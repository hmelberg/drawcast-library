Why can a heat pump deliver more heat than the electricity it uses? It sounds like something for nothing, so follow where the extra comes from.

On a mild day, plus seven outside, one kilowatt-hour of electricity puts about three kilowatt-hours of heat into the house.

The same refrigerant goes round and round these four parts, and the trick is what happens in each one.

Outside, the refrigerant is colder than the air, so heat flows into it.

That is where the missing two kilowatt-hours come from: the outdoor air.

The compressor squeezes the gas, and squeezing a gas makes it hot, hotter than the room. This is the step the electricity pays for.

Now the refrigerant is the hottest thing in the room, so heat flows out of it, into the air you breathe.

The valve lets it expand and turn cold again, ready to go back outside for another load.

Round it goes. The electricity never becomes most of the heat; it pays to carry heat that was already outside.

An electric heater turns one kilowatt-hour into exactly one. It has nothing to carry.

In frost there is still heat in the air, but the pump must lift it further, from minus fifteen up to room warmth, so each kilowatt-hour carries less.

At minus fifteen a typical air unit gives about two: still double a heater, but no longer three.

Lord Kelvin described such a heat multiplier in 1852, a century before heat pumps warmed ordinary homes.

At minus fifteen, where does the second kilowatt-hour come from?

The outdoor air: minus fifteen is far above absolute zero, and the refrigerant in the coil is colder still, so heat flows in.
