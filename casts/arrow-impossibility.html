Arrow's impossibility theorem

A town that votes in circles, the theorem that says no voting rule is perfect, and why that does not make democracy impossible.

Transcript

Here's a question that sounds simple: when people disagree, is there a fair way to turn everyone's rankings into one choice for the group?

Easy. Majority rule. Whatever most people prefer, the group prefers.

Let's test that. A town of three equal groups must build one thing: a pool, a park or a library.

Group one ranks pool, then park, then library. Group two: park, library, pool. Group three: library, pool, park.

Now vote in pairs. Pool against park: groups one and three put the pool higher, so it wins two to one.

Park against library: which wins? Tap it.

The park, two to one.

The park, two to one: groups one and two both rank it above the library.

So the pool beats the park, and the park beats the library. Then the pool must beat the library.

That's what logic says. But groups two and three both prefer the library, so it beats the pool, two to one.

The marquis de Condorcet spotted this in 1785: every voter is perfectly consistent, and yet the majority goes round in a circle.

That circle is our thread: who it hands power to, why Kenneth Arrow proved no voting rule fully escapes it, and how much it should worry us.

Round and round, then. So which one does the town actually build?

That depends on the order of the votes. Say the chair puts pool against park first: the pool wins, then meets the library, and loses.

Now you chair the meeting, and you want the park. Which option do you hold back for the final vote? Tap it.

Hold back the park: the library wins the first vote, and the park beats the library.

Hold the park back. Pool against library first, the library wins, and then the park beats the library. Whatever comes in last wins.

So whoever sets the order picks the winner, and every single vote along the way looks fair.

Could a cleverer rule fix it? In 1951 a young economist, Kenneth Arrow, asked whether any rule at all could do the job fairly.

He asked for very little. Any rankings are allowed. If everyone prefers pool to park, so does the group. And no dictator, whose ranking always wins.

The result must be a proper ranking, with no circles. And the verdict on pool against park can't depend on where people put the library.

That's the bare minimum. Any decent rule would pass.

Arrow proved that with three or more options, no rule meets all five at once. It's part of why he shared the 1972 Nobel prize.

What about points, then? Two for first place, one for second. No circles there.

No circles, but take five voters: three rank pool, park, library; two rank park, library, pool. On points the park wins, seven to six.

Now the library drops out. Nobody changes their mind about pool and park, yet the pool wins, three to two. Points give up independence.

So that's it: Arrow proved democracy is impossible.

That's the strong reading. In 1982 William Riker argued that votes can't reveal a will of the people; at best they let us throw rulers out.

But the theorem is narrower: no rule works for every possible pattern of rankings. It says nothing about how often the bad patterns turn up.

Take the gloomiest model: every voter picks a ranking at random, like rolling a die.

With three voters and three options, a circle turns up 12 times in 216, about 6 percent.

And with millions of voters, still three options? Set your guess.

Just under 9 percent. And with five options instead of three, about one in four.

But real voters aren't dice. Their views share patterns, and Michel Regenwetter and colleagues showed that pushes circles down; in surveys and ballots they found almost none.

And when Gerry Mackie rechecked the famous cases of cycling in 2003, Riker's among them, most of them didn't hold up.

So what keeps real electorates out of the circle?

Often, the options line up on one scale. Say a small, medium or large budget, and each voter likes options less the further they are from a favourite.

That shape is the rescue. Duncan Black showed in 1948 that then there's no circle: the voter in the middle, the median voter, is on the winning side of every pair.

And our town failed that test. Every option was someone's last choice, so no order of the three gives everyone a single peak.

Other rules step outside rankings altogether. In score or approval voting you grade each option, so Arrow's theorem doesn't apply; but grades must mean the same to everyone, and tactical voting still pays.

And politicians trade votes: you back my bridge, I'll back your hospital.

Log-rolling lets strong feelings count, which rankings can't. But it's no cure: vote trading is tied to circles too, and when everyone trades, everyone can end up worse off.

I'd have guessed small committees are where circles really lurk.

On pure chance, no: three voters circle a little less often than millions. But in a big electorate shared views average out and circles fade; a small committee gets no such protection.

And committees weigh issues with many sides at once. Richard McKelvey showed in 1976 that then majority rule can circle almost everywhere, so whoever sets the agenda can steer the vote nearly anywhere.

So Arrow doesn't say democracy is impossible. He says no ranking rule is perfect: each gives up one of these, or steps outside rankings altogether.

So choose what to give up, on purpose, and keep an eye on whoever sets the order.

A five-member board picks one of four sites by votes in pairs, and the chair sets the order. What should worry you most?

The order: if the sites form a circle, the one brought in last wins, and with four options a circle is more likely than with three.

So, a fair way to add up our rankings? Not a perfect one. But a good one, if we know what we gave up.
