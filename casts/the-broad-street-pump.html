The map that blamed a water pump

How John Snow traced London's 1854 cholera outbreak to one pump, and why the exceptions, not the cluster, made his case

How did one map point at a single water pump, and change how we think cholera spreads? It starts on 31 August 1854, in a few streets of Soho.

Within ten days, some five hundred people nearby were dead.

Most doctors blamed miasma, a poisonous bad air. John Snow suspected the water, and got the addresses of the dead.

Most had lived nearest one pump, in Broad Street. On 7 September he put the case to the parish board.

The next day, the handle of the Broad Street pump came off.

Only later did he draw each death as a short black bar on the street where it happened.

Here is that map, from the 1855 edition of Snow's book on how cholera spreads.

Every black bar is one death, stacked at its address, and the stacks crowd round one point near the middle: the Broad Street pump.

But a cluster alone fits bad air too: the air near the pump was as foul as its water. What settled it were the exceptions.

A brewery stood yards from the pump. Of its seventy or so workers, none died: they drank beer, and the brewery had its own well.

The workhouse nearby held 535 people, and only five died. It had its own well too.

And a widow in Hampstead, miles away, died of cholera. She liked the taste of Broad Street water, and had it carried to her.

Same air, other water: spared. Other air, same water: dead. The exceptions turn a cluster into a cause.

The story usually leaves out one thing: the outbreak was already fading when the handle came off. What the map did was make the argument, though it took years to win it.

Which finding best shows it was the water, not the air?

They breathed their neighbours' air but drank other water. A cluster fits bad air too, and the outbreak was already fading.
