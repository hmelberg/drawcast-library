Why most published findings are false

A thousand hypotheses in one table show how honest studies, low power and long-shot ideas fill journals with false findings.

How can most published findings be false when nobody cheats? Sort a thousand hypotheses a field might test: true or false, significant or not.

Say a hundred of the thousand are really true. That share, the prior, is the number no single study reports.

Studies with eighty percent power find eighty of the hundred true effects, and miss twenty.

The nine hundred false hypotheses face a five percent threshold, so forty-five come out significant anyway, by chance.

Journals print the significant column: a hundred and twenty-five findings.

Forty-five of them are false: more than one in three, though every study followed the rules.

Now make the studies small, with twenty percent power. Only twenty true effects surface, but the forty-five false ones stay.

Two in three significant findings are now false. Low power doesn't just miss effects; it makes the effects you do find less believable.

And take a field chasing long shots, where only ten hypotheses in a thousand are true, tested with good power again.

Eight true finds, fifty false ones: most of what it publishes is false.

John Ioannidis set out this arithmetic in 2005, in a paper whose title makes the claim outright.

So a significant result is only as believable as its field's prior and its study's power, and a p-value reports neither.

A field of long shots, tested with large studies: are its significant findings mostly true or mostly false?

Mostly false: when true hypotheses are rare, the five percent of false ones that slip through outnumber the real finds, whatever the power.
