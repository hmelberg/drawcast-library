What do sensitivity and specificity measure?

Why a test that is 90% sensitive and 95% specific can still be wrong about most positives when the disease is rare.

What do sensitivity and specificity actually tell you about a test? Test a thousand people, and sort them two ways.

Two hundred of them really have the disease. The test catches 180: true positives.

But it misses twenty: false negatives, sick people sent home reassured.

Of the 800 healthy people, 760 correctly test negative.

And forty test positive anyway: false positives, healthy people told they may be ill.

Sensitivity reads down the sick column: of those who have the disease, how many does the test catch?

180 of 200: 90 percent.

Specificity reads down the healthy column: of those without it, how many does it clear?

760 of 800: 95 percent.

Both are counted among people whose true state we already know. So they describe the test itself.

But a patient holding a positive result asks another question, reading across the row.

Of 220 positives, 180 are ill: 82 percent.

Now use the same test where only 20 in a thousand are ill.

It still catches 18 of them, and misses 2.

But 49 of the 980 healthy people test positive too.

So the row turns: of 67 positives, only 18 are ill. 27 percent.

Yet sensitivity has not moved: 18 of 20, still 90 percent.

Nor has specificity. The columns belong to the test; the row depends on how common the disease is.

Jacob Yerushalmy gave the two terms their meaning in 1947, comparing how well doctors read chest X-rays for tuberculosis.

The same test is used in a clinic where half the patients have the disease. What is its sensitivity?

Still 90 percent: sensitivity is counted only among the sick, so it does not depend on how many are sick. What changes is how often a positive is right.
