Do natural disasters kill more people than a century ago?

Today disasters kill about 45,000 people a year. Guess the 1920s toll, then see why the risk per person fell by about 98 percent.

Earthquakes, floods, storms, droughts: do they kill more people today than a hundred years ago?

In the 2010s, disasters killed about forty-five thousand people a year.

Drag the 1920s bar to your guess.

Most of it was drought, which in poor farming countries meant famine.

Drought deaths fell from about four hundred and seventy thousand a year to about two thousand, as farming, trade and food aid improved.

Bad years still come: in 2004 alone, disasters killed about a quarter of a million people, most of them in the Indian Ocean tsunami.

And there were only two billion people then, against seven and a half billion now. So count per million people.

Two hundred and sixty-six deaths per million people a year, down to about six.

A fall of about ninety-eight percent.

Today disasters cause less than a tenth of one percent of all deaths in most years.

So disasters still fill the news, but the chance of dying in one has never been smaller.
