Here's a question worth a million dollars: why does checking an answer seem so much easier than finding one?

Take eight numbers, and pick some that add up to exactly one hundred.

Easy: thirty-eight and sixty-three!

Do 38 and 63 make exactly 100?

No: thirty-eight plus sixty-three is a hundred and one. One addition, and you knew.

Checking is quick. Now try the other job: finding a pick that works. Tap the numbers that add up to exactly 100, then press Done.

That took me much longer. I kept adding, and throwing sums away.

That's the gap. Each check is one sum, but how many picks are there to check? How many different picks can you make from eight numbers?

Each number is either in or out: two choices, eight times over. Two to the eight is two hundred and fifty-six.

So a ninth number doubles the work?

Every new number doubles it. There are cleverer methods than trying every pick, but for long lists of big numbers, none is known that escapes this kind of growth.

Let's draw both kinds of work against the size of the problem, n. Checking a pick takes about n additions; call it n squared.

Why squared, if it's only n additions?

To show that even a slower check stays tame.

Searching every pick takes two to the n.

At thirty, n squared is nine hundred; two to the n is over a billion.

But computers are fast. A billion steps a second?

Then fifty numbers take about thirteen days; a hundred take about forty trillion years, some three thousand times the age of the universe.

Checking an answer for a hundred numbers, meanwhile: at most ten thousand steps, a hundred-thousandth of a second.

Computer scientists sort problems by exactly this. P: the problems we can solve fast, with steps growing like a power of n, never like two to the n.

NP is the problems where a proposed answer can be checked fast, like our sum.

So is every problem in P also in NP?

Is every problem in P also in NP?

Yes: if you can solve it fast, you can check an answer fast. Just solve it and compare.

The open question is whether anything in NP lies outside P.

In 1971 Stephen Cook, and independently Leonid Levin, found something remarkable.

Some problems in NP are the hardest of all: every NP problem can be translated into them, quickly.

Translated? How do you turn a sudoku into a sum?

Mechanically. Any NP puzzle's rules can be rewritten, fast, as an instance of the hard problem: solve that, and you've solved the original.

In 1972 Richard Karp showed twenty-one famous problems are like that. Today thousands are known, from map colouring to sudoku to our sum.

So if I found a fast method for just one of them…

…you'd have one for all of NP, and P would swallow NP.

That would be wonderful, in part: delivery routes, timetables and chip layouts could be optimised fast, if the method were practical.

But public-key encryption could fall, in principle: it relies on locks that are easy to check and hard to pick.

Is encryption one of those NP-complete problems?

No: it rests on problems like factoring, which isn't known to be NP-complete. But factoring is in NP, so if P equals NP, it could fall with the rest.

And mathematics? A computer could find proofs about as easily as it checks them?

For proofs of reasonable length, yes. But nobody knows whether P equals NP.

In a 2019 poll, eighty-eight percent of researchers bet it doesn't: decades of searching have found no fast method for any of those thousands.

Prove it either way, and the Clay Mathematics Institute pays a million dollars, a prize offered since 2000.

So checking stays easy as problems grow, and finding may not. And one fast method for any NP-complete problem would settle it for everything.

A friend hands you a filled-in sudoku. What is quick?

Checking: one look at every row, column and box. Solving can mean a long search.

Someone finds a fast method for three-colouring maps, an NP-complete problem. What follows?

Every NP problem translates quickly into map colouring, so a fast colourer solves them all: P would equal NP.
