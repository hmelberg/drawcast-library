Why almost-full hospital wards keep patients waiting

Why a hospital ward that is nine-tenths full keeps patients waiting so much longer than one that is half full — and why the last few beds matter most.

Why do waits for a hospital bed explode when a ward is almost, but not quite, full?

Surely a ward at 95 % full is only a little worse than one at 90? The wait just grows in step.

That's the natural guess. Take the simplest queue, with made-up numbers: say a patient waits an hour for a bed when the ward is half full.

Fill it to 80 %, and the wait is four hours; at 90 %, nine.

Doubled? Why would five points matter so much?

Because patients do not arrive evenly: some nights bring a rush, and only the beds still free can soak it up.

At 90 % full, a tenth of the capacity is spare; at 95 %, only a twentieth.

The wait follows busy divided by spare, so halving the spare share about doubles the wait.

What does that mean on a bad night, say five extra admissions?

A ward that can discharge twenty a day but takes in ten has ten spare a day, and catches up within half a day.

At 95 % full it takes in nineteen and has one spare a day: five days to catch up, and the next bad night comes first.

But a real ward has dozens of beds. Doesn't that change things?

It softens it: a ward with many beds stays flat for longer, but the wall at full stays where it is.

Even so, above about 85 % full the bad nights start leaving no bed free at all now and then: that is where a well-known 1999 study found the risks begin.

So it's not really how full the ward is.

No: it's how little room is left to absorb bad luck — and near full, that room vanishes fast.

If every patient arrived exactly on schedule, evenly spaced, and every stay lasted exactly as long, what would the wait be at 95 % full?

Hardly any: with no bad nights and no surprise stays, there is never a rush for the spare beds to absorb.

That is why some hospitals now spread planned operations evenly across the week: they cannot remove the bad nights, but they can stop adding their own.
