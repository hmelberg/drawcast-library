Surgery or medication?

How a decision tree weighs every outcome by its chance to pick between surgery and tablets, and how safe that choice really is.

Surgery or tablets? When both treatments are gambles, how does a decision tree choose? Here the same disease can be operated on or treated with medication.

The square is the choice we make. Choose surgery, and we reach a circle: from here on, chance decides.

Eighty-five times in a hundred the operation works, and the patient can expect nine quality-adjusted life years.

Fifteen times in a hundred there is a serious complication, and only three QALYs.

Medication is a gamble too, only a milder one.

Seven in ten are well controlled, at eight QALYs; the other three in ten get six.

Compare the best cases and surgery wins, nine to eight.

Compare the worst cases and medication wins, six to three. Neither comparison is the answer.

Instead, fold the tree back: weight each ending by its chance. Nine times 0.85 plus three times 0.15 gives surgery 8.1 expected QALYs.

Eight times 0.7 plus six times 0.3 gives medication 7.4.

Surgery, by 0.7 QALYs a patient, even though about one patient in seven ends up worse off than on tablets.

How safe is that verdict? Let the complication risk be p. Surgery keeps winning until p passes about 0.27.

So the question for the evidence is not the average risk, but whether it could plausibly be above one in four.

The complication risk turns out to be 0.2, not 0.15. Which option does the tree now pick?

Still surgery: nine times 0.8 plus three times 0.2 is 7.8, above medication's 7.4. The switch comes only past about 0.27.
