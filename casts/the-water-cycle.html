Why rain is fresh

How the sun distils salty seawater into fresh rain, and why the very same cycle has slowly made the sea salty.

Most rain starts in the salty sea, yet it falls fresh. Why? To find out, follow one drop round the water cycle, from the ocean to the mountain and back.

The engine is the sun. Its warmth pulls water off the sea surface as invisible vapour.

That is evaporation, and only the water goes. The salt cannot evaporate, so it stays behind in the sea.

So the sun works like a still the size of the oceans: over four hundred thousand cubic kilometres of fresh water rise off them each year.

Rising air cools, and the vapour condenses on specks of dust as tiny droplets: a cloud.

Wind carries it inland, over the high ground, where the droplets merge and grow.

Once they are too heavy for the rising air to hold, they fall as fresh rain: precipitation. A water molecule stays up there only about nine days.

On the slope the rain gathers into streams and then a river, running back toward the sea.

Some soaks into the ground instead, filling the pores of the rock below the water table.

That groundwater creeps seaward too, over years or even centuries.

Together they return about forty thousand cubic kilometres a year: the surplus the wind carried in over the land.

But on the way, rivers pick up a little salt, dissolved out of the rocks.

Evaporation never lifts it back, so over the ages the sea grew salty.

So one cycle does both jobs: it hands the land fresh water, and it leaves the sea its salt.

The Dead Sea has rivers flowing in but none flowing out. Why does it keep getting saltier?

Rivers bring a little salt in, and water leaves only by evaporating, so the salt piles up.
