Why a straw looks bent in water

Follow one ray of light out of the water to see why a straight straw looks broken, and why a pool's bottom looks shallower than it is.

Why does a straw standing in a glass of water look broken, right at the surface?

The straw itself is perfectly straight.

Follow the light from one point on it: its tip, under the water.

That light travels up toward the surface in a straight line.

Measured from the dashed line square to the surface, it arrives at thirty degrees.

Crossing into air, it bends away from that line.

It leaves at almost forty-two degrees.

Light moves about a third faster in air than in water, and the side of the beam that gets out first pulls ahead, swinging the beam round.

Now put your eye where that light lands.

Your brain assumes light travels in straight lines, so it traces the ray straight back.

That line points above the real tip: the tip looks shallower than it is.

Every point below the surface is lifted the same way, while the part in the air stays put, so the straw seems to kink at the surface.

The straw was never bent; the light was.

Look from a lower angle, and the tip seems to rise even higher.

Looking into a pool, where does a coin on the bottom seem to be?

Shallower: its light bends away from the normal as it leaves the water, and traced straight back it points above the coin.
