Why an epidemic turns by itself

How an epidemic peaks and fades while the virus stays just as contagious: it burns through the very people it needs

Why does an epidemic peak and then fade, even when nobody was immune at the start?

Take a virus where each case infects three others. This is the share of a town ill at once, in the simplest epidemic model.

It climbs, peaks near day thirty-eight, and drains away.

On day twenty, a case meets three people it could infect, and nearly all of them are still susceptible.

Three times the share still susceptible, 0.93: about 2.8 new cases for every case. So the wave grows.

At the peak, two of every three people have had it. The same three meetings find only one person who can still catch it.

Three times a third is one. Each case now just replaces itself, which is exactly why the curve stops rising here.

By day fifty-five, nine in ten have had it. Three meetings make about a third of a new case, and the wave drains.

Nothing about the virus changed. It never weakened, and nobody stayed home: three meetings per case, from the first day to the last.

What changed is who it met. The epidemic turns because it is using up its own fuel, making the immunity nobody had at the start.

It even stops short: about six in a hundred never catch it, because by the end there are too few cases left to reach them.

William Farr saw in 1840 that epidemics rise and fall in a regular curve, almost ninety years before this model explained why.

At the peak, why does each case infect only one other?

Two of its three contacts are already immune: the virus spreads as easily as ever, but only a third of the people it meets can still catch it.
