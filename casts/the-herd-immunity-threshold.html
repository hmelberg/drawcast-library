How many must be immune to stop a disease?

Why a disease twice as contagious needs only a little more immunity, and why measles needs 95 % of children vaccinated

Twice as contagious: does a disease then need twice as many people vaccinated before it stops?

The first knob is R0: how many people one case infects when nobody is immune.

To stop spreading, each case must infect fewer than one. With a share p immune, a case infects R0 times one minus p.

Solve for p: the immune share must pass one minus one over R0. That is the herd immunity threshold.

The other two knobs belong to the vaccination programme: how well the vaccine protects, and how many people get it.

The curve is that threshold for every R0.

At an R0 of two, the dot says half of us must be immune.

The dashed line is who is immune today: sixty percent vaccinated, nine in ten of them protected, so 54 percent. Just past the dot.

Walk R0 up. The share needed leaps from under forty percent to two thirds by an R0 of three, then only crawls: three quarters, four fifths.

So a disease twice as contagious does not need twice the coverage. From an R0 of two to four, the need goes from a half to three quarters.

Now measles, whose R0 is usually put at twelve to eighteen. At twelve, ninety-two percent must be immune.

Here is the catch. With a vaccine that protects nine in ten, even vaccinating everyone leaves us short: past an R0 of ten, it can never be enough.

Two doses of measles vaccine protect about ninety-seven percent. Given to ninety-five percent of children, that clears an R0 of twelve, though not quite eighteen.

That is why the World Health Organization asks for at least ninety-five percent coverage against measles, and why a small dip lets outbreaks back in.

Try it: pick an R0, then find the lowest coverage that clears the dot, first with a weak vaccine, then a strong one.

R0 doubles from 4 to 8. The share that must be immune…

From three quarters to seven eighths: one minus one over R0 flattens, so twice as contagious needs only a little more, though that little may be beyond a vaccine's reach.
