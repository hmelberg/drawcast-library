How a nerve signal travels

How a nerve cell fires an all-or-nothing spike, passes it down its axon like a burning fuse, and signals strength by rate.

How does a signal travel along a nerve cell? It starts at the dendrites, the branches that collect messages from other cells.

The cell body adds them up.

At rest, its inside sits at minus seventy millivolts, charged like a tiny battery.

If the inputs lift it to about minus fifty-five, the threshold, the neuron fires. Below that, nothing is sent at all.

Firing means sodium channels open, sodium ions rush in, and the inside flips to about plus thirty, for roughly a millisecond.

That spike happens first where the axon begins: the long output cable.

Now the inrush of charge lifts the next patch of membrane past threshold, so it fires too, then the next, like a fuse burning along.

A current in a wire fades with distance. But this signal doesn't: every patch rebuilds it to full size, so it arrives as strong as it left.

And it runs one way only. A patch that has just fired needs a moment to reset, so the wave cannot turn back.

Myelin speeds it up. It wraps the axon in insulation, so the spike is rebuilt only at the gaps, the nodes.

In between, it leaps from node to node, which is far faster.

At the terminals, finally, it releases chemical messengers that pass the signal to the next cell.

Every spike is the same size. So how does a neuron say 'stronger'?

It fires more often. A light touch sends a few spikes a second;

a firm press sends many more.

Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley worked this out in 1952, in a squid's giant axon, about half a millimetre thick. It won them a Nobel Prize.

You press harder on your fingertip. What changes in the signal its nerve sends?

More spikes per second: each spike is all or nothing, always the same size and speed, so strength is sent as how often it fires.
