Bread to Bolsheviks: Russia, 1917

How a bread strike toppled three centuries of Tsars in a week, and why Lenin's small party, not the crowd, held power by October.

This is Petrograd, the Russian capital, in 1917: women marching under their banners, so many that they fill the street from wall to wall.

They were marching for bread, and that February crowds like this one brought down a Tsar; yet by October, Lenin's Bolsheviks held power.

So how does a crowd asking for bread end with Lenin in charge?

Two revolutions, then, and under them three layers of cause: old cracks, fresh blows, and a spark for each.

The oldest cracks ran from the top. This is Nicholas the Second, around 1909; his own title called him autocrat, answerable to God alone.

Under the Tsar the old cracks ran deep: power answerable to no one, peasants hungry for land, and workers packed into new factories.

Then came the war. These are Russian prisoners at Augustów in February 1915; by 1917, millions had been killed, wounded or captured.

In 1915 Nicholas took command of the army himself, so every defeat carried his name; this is his headquarters at Mogilev, in September 1916.

Meanwhile the trains carried shells, not flour; these Petrograd women in 1917 are talking over their bread ration.

So two fresh blows landed on the old cracks: the war, and bread.

On the twenty-third of February, Women's Day, women like these walked out of the textile mills for bread; within two days the city was on strike.

Surely the army just shot them down?

Some units fired, on the twenty-sixth; the next day whole regiments mutinied, and armed workers and soldiers like these held the streets.

This is the Tauride Palace, the Duma's home, on the twenty-seventh: officers who had gone over to the new government crowd its hall.

Cut off from his capital, Nicholas gave up the throne on the second of March; three hundred years of Romanov rule had ended in about a week.

This is Nicholas months later, under guard; the night he gave up the throne he wrote, all around is treachery, cowardice and deceit.

With the Tsar gone, power now sat in two places. This is the Petrograd Soviet in 1917, a council of workers' and soldiers' deputies, and the soldiers obeyed it.

And the new Provisional Government? Ministers like Alexander Kerensky, here in 1917, kept Russia in the war.

So February's strike had brought the Tsar down; the third blow was a government in name, and a Soviet the soldiers obeyed.

Then, in April, Lenin came home.

He had spent the war in exile, in Zurich, and the way home ran through enemy country.

Germany, hoping he would knock Russia out of the war, let him cross in a sealed train, and a ferry took him on to Sweden.

This is Lenin in Stockholm that April, walking from the station with two Swedish socialists; that same evening he took the train north.

Round the top of the Baltic and down through Finland, he reached Petrograd's Finland Station on the night of the third of April.

And he brought three words a hungry, war-weary city understood: peace, land, bread.

At first few followed him, and the summer went badly for everyone.

In July, after a failed offensive at the front, armed crowds filled Nevsky Prospekt; on the fourth, government troops opened fire.

The Bolsheviks were blamed for days like this one; called German agents, Lenin went into hiding in Finland.

But the next threat came from the right. This is General Lavr Kornilov, the army's commander, in Moscow that August; soon after, he turned troops toward Petrograd.

Kerensky armed the workers, and Bolshevik Red Guards like these took up rifles; the troops were stopped before they arrived.

Now the Bolsheviks looked like the city's defenders, and in September they won the Petrograd Soviet.

And so to October. These are Red Guards in Petrograd that autumn, armed workers waiting for the word.

On the night of the twenty-fourth of October they took the bridges, the telegraph and the stations.

A British artist, Barnard Lintott, painted the crowds at the Winter Palace; in fact the last ministers were simply arrested inside, after midnight, and few died.

Add Kornilov's failed march as a fourth blow, and the October rising: the next day Lenin's decrees promised the soldiers peace and the peasants land.

So the crowd that wanted bread toppled the Tsar, but took no power for itself.

The government that inherited power kept the war, and lost the soldiers, the peasants and the bread queues, one by one.

Lenin's small party was the only one promising what the crowd had asked for, and ready to act when everyone else was spent.

Here they are once more, the women who marched for bread. February belonged to crowds like these; October belonged to a party that knew what it wanted, and took it.
