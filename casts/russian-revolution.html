Why the Tsar fell, and why Lenin won

Why Russia's Tsar collapsed in a week in 1917, and how a small, organised party took power eight months later by promising peace, land and bread.

Russia, 1917. In one year it loses its Tsar, and ends up ruled by Lenin. How?

That's two puzzles, really: why the Tsar fell, and why the Bolsheviks, of all people, won.

Isn't it one story? Lenin's revolutionaries overthrew the Tsar.

That's the usual picture, and it's wrong. When the Tsar fell, Lenin was in Switzerland, reading about it in the papers.

Start with the long fuse. In 1861 the serfs were freed, but they had to pay for their land over forty-nine years.

From the 1890s Russia industrialised fast. Peasants crowded into St Petersburg's factories, poorly paid and close to the throne.

Over it all sat one man with absolute power: Nicholas the Second, Tsar from 1894, sure that God had chosen him.

In January 1905 troops fired on a peaceful march to his palace. Bloody Sunday.

A year of strikes and mutinies followed, and Nicholas gave way just enough: an elected parliament, the Duma.

So Russia became a constitutional monarchy?

On paper. He dissolved the first two Dumas within months, and kept the final word.

Fine, that's the fuel. What lit it?

The war. By the end of 1916, around five million Russian soldiers had been killed, wounded or captured, and Nicholas had taken command himself.

Prices had roughly quadrupled, the railways carried soldiers before grain, and the capital ran short of bread and fuel.

On the twenty-third of February 1917, Women's Day, women textile workers walked out. Within days the city was on strike.

Wait. February? I always heard March.

Both are right. Russia still kept the old calendar, thirteen days behind the West's.

Then the turning point: on the twenty-seventh, the soldiers sent to put the crowds down joined them instead.

His generals told Nicholas the army would not back him, and on the second of March he abdicated.

So nobody planned it. Everything gave way at once.

Right, and the real puzzle is the next eight months. Power was now split. A Provisional Government of liberals, and the Petrograd Soviet, a council of workers' and soldiers' delegates.

And whom did the soldiers obey?

The Soviet. Its Order Number One said: obey the government only when the Soviet agrees. Lenin called it dual power.

Then the government made its fateful choice: stay in the war, and leave the land to a future assembly.

Why on earth stay in?

Loyalty to the allies, fear of Germany, and loans from Britain and France. The trenches didn't care.

In April, Lenin arrived at the Finland Station. Germany had let him cross by train, hoping he'd take Russia out of the war.

By autumn they had roughly ten times as many.

They grew because Lenin said what everyone wanted to hear: all power to the Soviets, and peace, land, bread.

In July, after a failed offensive, armed soldiers and sailors filled the streets. The revolt was crushed, and Lenin fled to Finland.

So that's the end of Lenin, then?

Not quite. In late August the army's chief, General Kornilov, sent troops toward the capital.

Kerensky, now heading the government, armed the workers and let jailed Bolsheviks out to stop him. Kornilov's march fell apart.

By September they led the Petrograd Soviet. On the twenty-fifth of October, our seventh of November, their Red Guards took the capital and, overnight, the Winter Palace.

Surely by then most Russians were behind them.

So the Tsar fell because everything gave way at once. But Lenin won because…

…his party was organised, and it promised peace and land now, while the moderates kept saying later.

Suppose in May 1917 the government had made peace and let the peasants take the land. What happens to the Bolsheviks' chances?

They lose their best argument: peace and land now was what drew soldiers and peasants to Lenin, and the moderates had refused it.
