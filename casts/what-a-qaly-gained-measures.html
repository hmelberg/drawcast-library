What does a 'QALY gained' actually count?

How a QALY weighs each year of life by its quality, and why easing suffering can count as much as adding years.

When a treatment is said to gain QALYs, what exactly is being counted? Let's draw it.

Time runs across. Quality of life runs up, from zero, dead, to one, full health.

A patient has just been diagnosed. Without treatment, quality of life starts at 0.6, declines, and the patient dies after eight years.

Each year counts by its quality: a year at 0.6 is 0.6 of a healthy year. The area under the curve, 3.8 here, is the QALYs left.

With treatment, the first months are worse: side effects push quality down to 0.3. Then it rises to 0.7, and the patient lives twelve years.

That dip is a real loss, shaded red: for a few months the treated patient is worse off.

But the green is a gain, and it comes in two kinds.

First, better years: for eight years, net of the red dip, almost one QALY.

Then, extra years. But they are lived at quality 0.5, so four extra years count as two QALYs, not four.

Together, about three QALYs gained: 6.65 with treatment against 3.8 without.

So a QALY gained is never just years added: easing pain for years can gain as much as extending life.

The name dates from a 1976 paper by the economists Richard Zeckhauser and Donald Shepard.

A drug adds four years of life, all lived at quality 0.25. How many QALYs does it add?

One: four years times 0.25. A QALY weighs every year by how good it is.
