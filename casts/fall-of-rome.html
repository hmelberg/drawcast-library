Why did the Western Roman Empire collapse — and why only the western half?

Easy. Lead pipes. They poisoned themselves.

Lovely theory. But the East had the same pipes, and it lasted another thousand years.

In 1984 the German historian Alexander Demandt listed every cause people had blamed: two hundred and ten of them.

Then I'll take two more off the menu: orgies, and Christianity. Gibbon blamed that one.

Gibbon did, in part. But two hundred and ten causes means nobody has found the one cause. So let's follow the money.

Around the year 200 the empire ringed the Mediterranean, from northern Britain to the Euphrates: a sea the Romans just called

Mare Nostrum

, our sea.

Then, from 235 to 284, it nearly fell apart: civil wars, plague, and silver coins with hardly any silver left.

So how many would-be emperors does fifty years of chaos get you?

And most recognised emperors died violently, usually at the hands of their own soldiers. In 238 alone there were six.

Six in one year. That's not a throne, that's a revolving door.

Diocletian, emperor from 284, shared out the rule; in 330 Constantine built a new capital in the East, Constantinople, today's Istanbul; and after 395 the split was for good.

Same empire, two halves. Which half would you rather inherit?

The East, every time. Egypt, Syria and Asia Minor had the richest cities and farms, and paid the most tax.

The West had the exposed frontier, the Rhine and the upper Danube; the East had the lower Danube and Persia to watch, and its rich heartland sat behind the sea and Constantinople's walls.

Fine. But in the end the barbarians just walked in, right?

Not quite walked. In 376 the Huns, horsemen from the steppe, drove Goths to the Danube, and the Goths asked to be let in.

Two years later, at Adrianople, those Goths destroyed an eastern Roman army and killed its emperor, Valens. Later, under Alaric, they moved west.

In 410 the Visigoths sacked Rome itself, the first time in almost eight hundred years.

So that's the fall, then. Roll credits.

Not yet. Rome wasn't even the capital any more: the western court sat in Ravenna, safe behind its marshes.

The blow that really hurt was a loss of land. Which loss do you think hurt the West most?

Tap the one you think hurt most.

Africa. In 439 the Vandals took Carthage, and with it the West's richest tax-paying provinces and much of Rome's grain.

Vandals. The name says it all.

A bit unfair: the word vandalism was only coined in 1794. But yes, in 455 they sailed over and looted Rome for two weeks.

So follow the money. An empire is a machine for turning taxes into soldiers, and without Africa the West couldn't pay enough of them.

So it hired whole barbarian peoples as allies — federates — who fought under their own kings.

Hiring the people you're fighting. What could go wrong?

Plenty. Allies were paid in land, and land handed over paid no more taxes.

So: less money, a weaker army, more deals, less land. A spiral.

The East had better money. It could pay its own armies, and even pay attackers like Attila to look elsewhere.

By 476 the West was a shell. A general called Odoacer deposed the last western emperor, a boy.

Romulus Augustulus, right? Rome's founder and its first emperor in one name, and they called him little Augustus.

Odoacer didn't even bother making himself emperor. He sent the imperial regalia east and ruled Italy as king, nominally under Constantinople.

The East went on for almost another thousand years, until the Ottomans took Constantinople in 1453.

So the Roman Empire didn't fall. Half of it just moved to Istanbul.

Historians still argue about the word fall. Some see a slow transformation; Bryan Ward-Perkins points to cruder pots, fewer coins, even smaller cattle.

But our answer is no single cause. The West lost its money, then its army, then its grip; the East kept all three.

Around 450, which would have done most to save the West?

Winning back Africa: its taxes paid for armies. East and West tried in 468 with over a thousand ships, and lost the fleet off Cape Bon.
