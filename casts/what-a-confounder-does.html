What a confounder does

Coffee, smoking and heart disease in one causal diagram: how a common cause fakes a link, and how comparing like with like removes it.

How can coffee look linked to heart disease without causing it? That is what a confounder does, and here is what adjusting for it undoes.

In a study, 14 percent of coffee drinkers get heart disease within ten years, against 8 percent of non-drinkers: a risk ratio of 1.75.

The tempting reading is this arrow: coffee raises the risk by three quarters.

But coffee drinkers smoke more. Here, 60 percent of them smoke, against 20 percent of non-drinkers.

Smoking goes with the coffee, and it causes heart disease. A common cause of both is what makes it a confounder.

Smokers bring their higher risk into the coffee group. That path, round the back through smoking, carries a link without any effect of coffee.

Adjusting means comparing like with like. Among smokers, 20 percent get heart disease, coffee or not. Among non-smokers, 5 percent.

Within each group the risk ratio is 1. The whole 1.75 came from mixing groups with different shares of smokers.

Holding smoking fixed closes the back door: within a group, smoking can no longer differ between drinkers and non-drinkers.

What remains is the arrow we set out to test, and in this example it was never there.

Coffee really was suspected of heart disease in the 1970s, until the Framingham study found the link was carried by smoking.

So adjust for a common cause like smoking: holding it fixed closes the back door it opens.

Suppose coffee raised blood pressure, and high blood pressure caused heart disease. Should the study adjust for blood pressure?

No: blood pressure would be a step on coffee's own path, so holding it fixed would block the very effect the study wants to measure.
