What do mitochondria actually do?

How mitochondria burn food with oxygen to make fifteen times more ATP, the fuel every cell spends, on folds full of tiny turbines

What do mitochondria actually do? They turn food into the one fuel a cell can spend, a molecule called ATP. Here is an animal cell.

The nucleus holds the instructions, but everything the cell does, building, moving, pumping, is paid for in ATP.

Follow one molecule of sugar, glucose, arriving from your last meal.

Out here in the cytoplasm, the cell can split it without any oxygen, and earn just two ATP.

The pieces then go into this bean-shaped compartment: a mitochondrion.

Inside, burned with oxygen all the way to carbon dioxide and water, the same glucose yields about thirty ATP.

Fifteen times as much. That gap is why you breathe: without oxygen, a cell is stuck with the two.

The trick is in its inner membrane, folded back and forth into ridges called cristae.

Look closer at one fold: a thin double membrane.

Electrons from food pass down a chain of proteins sitting in it.

Their energy pumps protons across, piling them up on one side like water behind a dam.

There is one way back: through a tiny turbine in the membrane, called ATP synthase.

As the protons rush back through, it really spins.

Each turn makes ATP, and more folds hold more turbines.

A mitochondrion even has its own DNA: it descends from a bacterium that moved into another cell over a billion years ago.

So a cell that works hard carries many. In heart muscle, which never rests, mitochondria fill about a third of each cell.

The oxygen you breathe in: where does it end up?

At the end of the chain, oxygen takes up the spent electrons with hydrogen. The carbon dioxide comes from the food.
