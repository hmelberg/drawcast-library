What a derivative is

How a speed at one instant can make sense: shrink the gap between two moments and watch the slope settle on the derivative.

What is a derivative? The rate of change at one instant — which sounds impossible. Take a dropped stone: after t seconds it has fallen about five t squared metres.

Two seconds in, it has fallen twenty metres. How fast is it moving at exactly that moment?

Speed needs two moments. At four seconds it has fallen eighty metres, and the line through both points has a slope…

…of sixty metres fallen over two seconds…

…which is thirty metres a second, on average.

But the stone kept speeding up after two seconds, so thirty is too fast for the moment we asked about.

Close the gap to one second: twenty-five metres more, so twenty-five metres a second.

Shrink it further — a tenth, a hundredth of a second — and the slope stops changing, closing in on twenty.

That limit is the derivative: twenty metres a second, the slope of the tangent — the line that just grazes the curve at two seconds.

Why not set the gap to zero? Then the stone falls zero metres in zero seconds, and zero over zero says nothing.

So the derivative is not a slope measured at one point, but the number the slopes head for as the gap shrinks.

A speedometer shows exactly this: never distance over a whole trip, only where that ratio is heading right now.

Newton wrote a dot over the letter, Leibniz wrote d y over d x, and the prime up there is Lagrange's: three names for one limit.
