What 95% confidence means

Twenty trials, twenty intervals, one miss: what the 95 percent in a confidence interval promises — and what it doesn't.

What does '95 percent confidence' actually promise? Less than most people think. Suppose a pill truly lowers blood pressure by 5 points: this dashed line.

Nobody gets to see that line. A trial measures some patients and reports an interval: here, 3.4 to 7.0 points.

A second trial, with other patients, lands somewhere else, and so does the next. The truth stays put; the intervals move.

Run twenty trials, and most of the intervals straddle the dashed line…

…but this one, from 2.1 to 4.6, misses it entirely. No mistake by its authors, just an unlucky sample.

Nineteen of twenty caught the truth. That catch rate, over many repeats, is all that 95 percent means.

From the inside, the red trial looks exactly like the others: its authors are just as confident, and wrong.

So the 95 percent belongs to the method, not to any one interval. Once computed, an interval either holds the 5 or it doesn't.

'A 95 percent chance the truth is in my interval' pictures the truth wandering. The truth is fixed; it's the intervals that wander.

Jerzy Neyman introduced confidence intervals in the 1930s, and defined them exactly this way: by how often the method catches the truth.

A trial reports a 95% interval of 2 to 8 points. What does the 95% describe?

The method: across many trials, 95 in 100 such intervals catch the true effect. This one either does or doesn't, and nobody can tell which.
