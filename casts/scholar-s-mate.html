Scholar's Mate: the four-move trap

How Scholar's Mate wins in four moves by counting attackers on one weak square, f7, and the single pawn move that stops it.

Scholar's Mate wins a game of chess in four moves. Why do so many players fall for it? It starts from the opening position.

e4: White's king pawn steps forward, and opens a door for the queen and the king's bishop at once.

e5: Black mirrors it.

Bishop to c4 looks like ordinary development, a piece stepping toward the centre.

But it aims at f7, the one square in Black's camp that only the king defends.

Knight to c6: Black develops and guards the pawn on e5, watching the centre rather than the corner.

Then the queen swings out to h5, aimed at that same square.

Count them: two attackers on f7, one defender. On a square, the side with more pieces bearing on it wins it.

One pawn move would do it: g6 cuts the queen's line to f7 and chases her away.

But the pawn stays where it is.

Knight to f6 instead, attacking the queen. It feels active, and it answers the wrong threat.

Queen takes f7: checkmate.

The king cannot take her back, because the bishop has guarded that square since move two.

So the trap is arithmetic on one square. Black watched the queen, when what mattered was how many pieces bore on f7.

After Qh5, Black plays Qe7 instead of Nf6. Is f7 still lost?

No: the queen on e7 also guards f7. After Qxf7+ Qxf7 Bxf7+ Kxf7, White has traded queen and bishop for queen and pawn: a bishop down.
