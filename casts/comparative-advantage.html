Why trade if you're better at everything?

How Ricardo's wine and cloth show that trade pays even a country that is better at making both goods than its partner.

Why would a country that makes everything more cheaply still buy from abroad? The answer came in 1817, from David Ricardo, a rich stockbroker turned economist.

His example: two countries, two goods, and the hours of work each needs for one unit.

Portugal needs eighty hours for a unit of wine, ninety for a unit of cloth.

England needs more for both: a hundred and twenty, and a hundred. Portugal is better at everything.

So why would Portugal buy anything from England? Because the real price of wine is the cloth you don't make.

In Portugal, eighty hours of wine could have made eight ninths of a cloth. Wine is cheap there.

In England, a hundred and twenty hours of wine could have made one point two cloth. Wine is dear there — so cloth is relatively cheap.

Now Portugal moves ninety hours out of cloth: one cloth less, and an eighth more than one wine.

England moves a hundred and twenty hours out of wine: one wine less, and one point two cloth more.

Same hours, same workers — and together they have more of both goods. Trade then shares the extra out.

What counts is not who is faster, but who gives up less. Even a country worse at everything is relatively best at something.

Asked for one idea in social science that is both true and not obvious, Paul Samuelson named this one.

When would trade between the two countries gain nothing?

When both give up the same cloth per wine: with equal opportunity costs, no switch of hours makes more of anything.
