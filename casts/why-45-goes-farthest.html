Why 45° goes farthest

Split a throw into speed across and time aloft to see why 45 degrees wins, and why 30° and 60° land on the very same spot.

Why does a ball launched at 45 degrees go farthest? At a fixed speed, the angle alone decides. Here, 20 metres a second, at 45.

Split the launch velocity in two: about fourteen metres a second across, and fourteen up.

Gravity takes 10 metres a second off the upward part every second. At the top, 1.4 seconds in, only the 14 across is left.

It lands 2.8 seconds after launch, as fast as it left, the upward part now pointing down.

The range is the speed across times the time in the air: fourteen times 2.8, about forty metres.

The angle trades one against the other. Keep the speed, and tilt the launch.

At seventy degrees it climbs high and stays up longer, but crawls across: it lands at about twenty-six metres.

Now lower it. At 45 the range peaks, out at forty metres,

then shrinks again: at 20 degrees it races across but drops within 1.4 seconds.

Twenty-six metres again, the same spot. Any two angles that add up to ninety land together.

In a formula, the range goes with sine of twice the angle, which peaks at ninety, when the angle is forty-five.

Galileo proved both results in 1638: forty-five goes farthest, and angles equally above and below it land together.

Real air drags hardest on long, high flights, so outside the textbook the best angle is somewhat lower.

At this speed, a ball launched at 30° lands 35 metres away. Which other angle lands at the same spot?

Sixty degrees: thirty and sixty add up to ninety, so what one angle gains in time aloft the other gains in speed across.
