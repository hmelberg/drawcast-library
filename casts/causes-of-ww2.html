From peace to war, 1919–1939

How a resented peace, a weak League and a slump made war possible in Europe, and why Hitler's unanswered gambles made it come in 1939.

In twenty years Europe went from a peace treaty to a second world war. How?

The usual answer is Versailles: a peace so harsh that it made the next war.

It was harsh: Germany lost thirteen per cent of its land and a tenth of its people.

One clause blamed it for the war, and it was billed 132 billion gold marks.

So Germany was crushed.

That's the myth. It paid about twenty billion before payments stopped in 1931.

And it was still the biggest power in the middle of Europe, among small new states. Angry, but not weak.

Then who was meant to keep the peace?

The League of Nations. But the United States never joined, and the League had no army of its own.

In 1931 Japan seized Manchuria, and when the League objected, Japan simply left. In 1935 Mussolini's Italy invaded Abyssinia, and the sanctions spared oil.

Then came the crash of 1929. By 1932 six million Germans were out of work.

In 1928 the Nazis had won just 2.6 per cent of the vote. What share did they win in July 1932? Click on the line.

Desperate voters turned to the extremes, and in January 1933 Hitler became chancellor.

His aims were no secret: tear up Versailles, bring all Germans into one Reich, and win living space,

Lebensraum

, in the east.

So a resented treaty, a toothless League, a slump, and a man who wanted war. That made war certain.

Possible, not certain. Every step from here was a gamble that someone could have stopped.

Here is the middle of Europe in 1935: Germany, with Austria and Czechoslovakia to the south, and Poland to the east.

March 1935: Hitler brought back conscription, openly breaking the treaty. There were protests, and nothing more.

March 1936: German troops marched into the Rhineland, the strip the treaty kept empty of soldiers.

Couldn't France just march in?

France could have. Hitler later said those forty-eight hours were the most nerve-racking of his life. France did nothing.

So why did Britain and France keep giving way?

Memory, first: more than two million of their men had died in 1914 to 1918.

Their generals said they were not ready, and many hoped his aims were limited: Germans rejoining Germany. That policy was appeasement.

And did giving way work?

Not for long. March 1938: German troops crossed into Austria, and it was annexed, the

Anschluss.

September 1938, at Munich: Britain and France handed him the Sudetenland, the German-speaking rim of Czechoslovakia.

And Chamberlain flew home promising peace for our time.

Six months later, in March 1939, Hitler took the rest of the Czech lands. These were Czechs, not Germans, and the hope of limited aims died.

Britain now promised to defend Poland. Hitler's answer, in August: a pact with Stalin to carve Poland up between them.

On the first of September Germany invaded Poland. On the third, Britain and France declared war.

Surely he'd expected that?

He had bet they wouldn't. A week before, he told his generals he had seen his enemies at Munich, and called them worms.

Some causes made war possible; others made it come in 1939. Sort each cause into its box.

So did Hitler plan all this?

Historians still argue. A. J. P. Taylor called him an opportunist; most now say he had aims, and took each chance he was given.

The long causes built the danger; what turned it into war was a lesson, taught step by step.

So every step that went unanswered told him the next one was safe.

Until Poland, when it wasn't. And by then, stopping him meant a world war.

Suppose France had marched into the Rhineland in March 1936. On this argument, what was most likely?

He pulls back: his own generals expected to retreat, and a bluff that fails makes the next one look dangerous.
