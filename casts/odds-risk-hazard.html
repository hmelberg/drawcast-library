Odds, risks and hazards

How risk, odds and hazard ratios differ, when they agree, and why twice the odds is rarely twice the risk, worked through a stroke trial, a lung-cancer case-control study and survival curves.

A paper can report a treatment's effect as a risk ratio, an odds ratio or a hazard ratio. They sound alike, but they answer different questions, and the gap between them can be large.

All of them start from the same place, a two-by-two table of exposure against outcome.

Take a made-up trial. A hundred patients get the drug, and ten of them have a stroke within a year.

Another hundred get placebo, and twenty of them have a stroke.

The plainest measure is the risk, the share of the people at the start who have the event.

So the risk is ten percent on the drug, and twenty percent on placebo.

A risk without a time window means nothing, so we say one-year risk.

Subtract one risk from the other, and you get the risk difference, ten fewer strokes per hundred patients treated.

Turn it upside down, and it becomes the number needed to treat. Treat ten people for a year, and you prevent one stroke.

Laupacis, Sackett and Roberts proposed that number in nineteen eighty-eight, because it speaks in patients rather than proportions.

The ninety who were spared on the drug, and the eighty on placebo, will matter soon, because the next measure uses them.

For now, keep one idea. The risk difference and the number needed to treat are absolute measures. They tell you how many people a treatment changes.

Back in our trial, the one-year risk was twenty percent on placebo and ten on the drug.

Divide the risks instead of subtracting them, and you get the risk ratio, also called the relative risk. Here it is one half.

A risk ratio of one means no difference. Below one, the exposure prevents events, and above one, it causes them.

Reported as a relative risk reduction, that becomes the headline. The drug halves the risk of stroke.

Now keep the drug's effect the same, but give it to patients whose baseline risk is ten times lower.

Two in a hundred of them have a stroke on placebo. If the risk ratio holds, the drug halves that to one in a hundred.

The relative effect is still one half. But the absolute benefit has shrunk from ten percentage points to one.

To prevent one stroke in these patients, you must now treat a hundred people instead of ten, with the costs and side effects borne by all of them.

That is the usual division of labour. Risk ratios tend to be fairly stable across populations, so they travel well, while the risk difference depends on the baseline risk.

But it is the absolute effect that a patient's decision rests on.

One more property is easy to forget. A risk cannot exceed one, so when the baseline risk is sixty percent, no risk ratio can be above one point six seven.

That ceiling is a first hint that ratios behave differently when outcomes are common, and the odds ratio is where it matters most.

Next chapter: Odds

Risk is not the only way to put a number on a chance. Gamblers use odds, and so does logistic regression.

The odds compare the chance that an event happens with the chance that it does not.

A risk of twenty percent is twenty against eighty, odds of one to four, or zero point two five.

A risk of one half gives even odds, exactly one.

And a risk of eighty percent is eighty against twenty, odds of four.

To go back, divide the odds by one plus the odds. Four over five is eighty percent again.

So the odds run from zero to infinity, and they climb ever more steeply as the risk approaches one.

Now set the risk itself beside them, as a straight line.

Down where the risk is small, the two almost coincide. A risk of ten percent has odds of zero point one one.

That is the rare-outcome rule. When the event is rare, odds and risk are nearly the same number.

Higher up they part fast. At a risk of eighty percent, the odds are five times larger.

In our trial, the odds of stroke were ten to ninety on the drug, and twenty to eighty on placebo.

Placebo sits right here, at a risk of twenty percent and odds of a quarter. The next step is to divide one odds by the other.

Divide one group's odds by the other's, and you have the odds ratio, the measure behind case-control studies and logistic regression.

In the cells of a two-by-two table it is a times d over b times c, the cross-product ratio.

For our stroke trial it comes to zero point four four, a little further from one than the risk ratio of one half.

The same quantity sits at the heart of logistic regression, which models the log of the odds as a straight line in the covariates.

Each coefficient is a log odds ratio. For the drug in our trial it is about minus zero point eight one. Exponentiate it, and you are back at zero point four four.

So whenever a paper reports a logistic model, its effects are odds ratios, whatever the abstract calls them.

Its real strength shows when a disease is rare and would take decades to follow. A case-control study starts from the disease, not from the exposure.

The classic case is Doll and Hill's study of lung cancer, published in nineteen fifty. Of six hundred and forty-nine men with lung cancer in London hospitals, all but two had smoked.

Each was matched with a control patient without cancer. Among those six hundred and forty-nine men, twenty-seven had never smoked.

Now try to compute a risk. Six hundred and forty-seven cancers among twelve hundred and sixty-nine smokers looks like a risk of fifty-one percent.

But that number is an artefact. Take two controls per case and it drops to about a third, because the investigators chose how many controls there were.

The odds ratio does not care. Six hundred and forty-seven times twenty-seven, over six hundred and twenty-two times two, is about fourteen.

Doubling the controls doubles b and d alike, and the factor cancels in a times d over b times c.

The odds ratio is symmetric. The odds of smoking in cases against controls equal the odds of cancer in smokers against non-smokers.

Jerome Cornfield showed this in nineteen fifty-one, with lung cancer among his examples, and added that when the disease is rare, the odds ratio also approximates the risk ratio.

With only two non-smoking cases, fourteen is a rough estimate. But no plausible error brings it anywhere near one.

So when does the odds ratio stop approximating the risk ratio? The answer depends on how common the outcome is.

Fix the odds ratio at two, the same in every population, and let the baseline risk run from zero to one.

For a baseline risk p-zero, the matching risk ratio follows from this formula, which Zhang and Yu popularised in nineteen ninety-eight.

Drawn out, the risk ratio starts at two when the outcome is rare, and sinks toward one as it becomes common.

At a baseline risk of five percent, the risk ratio is still one point nine, and the odds ratio is a fair stand-in.

At fifty percent it is only one point three three. Twice the odds is a third more risk.

The odds ratio always lies further from one than the risk ratio, above one and below it, and the gap grows with the baseline risk.

Now turn the same picture over, to an odds ratio below one.

Set the odds ratio to zero point six. Where the outcome is common, the risk ratio climbs almost to one.

That is the scale of a real mix-up. In nineteen ninety-nine, a study reported that doctors referred Black patients for cardiac catheterization with an odds ratio of zero point six, compared with white patients.

News reports said Black patients were forty percent less likely to be referred. But the referral rates were eighty-five and ninety-one percent.

That is a risk ratio of about zero point nine three, a seven percent difference, as Schwartz, Woloshin and Welch pointed out in the same journal.

One warning about the conversion. Fed an adjusted odds ratio, it gives biased estimates and wrong confidence intervals, so with a common outcome, estimate the risk ratio directly, with a log-binomial or a modified Poisson model.

So with a common outcome, read an odds ratio as an exaggerated risk ratio, in whichever direction it points.

Next chapter: Time

Risks and odds both count people. But in a real cohort, people are followed for different lengths of time.

Here are six patients followed after a first heart attack, an illustration. Three have a second one, after one, two and three years.

The others leave without it. The study ends, or they move away, and their follow-up is censored.

Three events among six people looks like a risk of fifty percent. But over what time? One patient was watched for a year, others for five.

The fix is to count time instead of people. Add up every year each patient was watched.

These six contribute twenty person-years between them.

Three events in twenty person-years is a rate of zero point one five per person-year, or fifteen per hundred.

Two groups are compared by their rate ratio, which, like the risk ratio, is one when the exposure does nothing.

Ten events in five hundred person-years against ten in two hundred and fifty is a rate ratio of one half, even though the counts are equal.

With a constant rate, a rate turns back into a risk. After five years at zero point one five, the risk is one minus e to the minus zero point seven five, about fifty-three percent.

Person-time has one assumption built in. The censored patients must be like those who stayed, because if the sickest drop out, the rate looks better than it is.

Now imagine counting events per person-time in an ever thinner slice of time. In the limit, the rate at a single instant is called the hazard.

Shrink that slice of time to an instant, and the rate becomes the hazard, the event rate among those still event-free, right now.

Take patients with an advanced cancer, and say that on standard treatment they die at a constant hazard of zero point two a year, an illustration.

Drawn as the share who have died, their curve rises as one minus e to the minus zero point two t.

The hazard ratio compares two groups' hazards at the same moment.

Now add a new drug, and start it at a hazard ratio of one, where its curve lies on top of the standard one.

Then lower the hazard ratio to one half. At every moment, patients on the new drug die at half the standard rate.

This is the Cox proportional hazards model, from nineteen seventy-two. The baseline hazard is left unspecified, and because it cancels in the ratio, the hazard ratio needs no model of time at all.

Now the trap. After two years, thirty-three percent of the standard group have died, against eighteen percent on the new drug.

After ten years it is eighty-six against sixty-three percent, a risk ratio of zero point seven three.

The hazard ratio stayed at one half the whole time, yet the risk ratio drifted toward one.

Everyone dies in the end. A drug that halves the hazard delays deaths, so the share who have died converges.

Under proportional hazards, survival on the new drug is simply the standard group's survival raised to the power of the hazard ratio.

Hernán added a subtler warning in twenty ten. Later hazards compare the survivors, and after the first deaths, the survivors in two arms are no longer alike, even in a randomized trial.

The Cox model assumes the hazard ratio is the same at every moment. Often it is not.

Take a made-up comparison. A medical treatment leaves patients with a steady death rate of eight percent a year.

Surgery kills some patients early, around the operation, but leaves the survivors with a hazard of only three percent a year.

In the first months the surgical hazard is far higher, so the hazard ratio is well above one.

Later it settles near zero point four, and the survival curves cross, here at four years.

That breaks the proportional hazards assumption.

A single Cox hazard ratio would average an early harm and a late benefit, weighted by when the deaths happen, and describe neither.

To check the assumption, plot log minus log survival against log time and look for parallel lines, or test the Schoenfeld residuals for a trend.

When it fails, report the curves themselves, hazard ratios for separate periods, or the restricted mean survival time, the area under each curve up to a fixed horizon.

Up to four years, surgery loses area, about three tenths of a year of life on average.

After four years it gains it back, just over half a year.

Over ten years, the net difference is about a fifth of a year, roughly ten weeks, in favour of surgery.

That number is in time, not in ratios, and it stays meaningful whether hazards are proportional or not.

Next chapter: Choosing a measure

One more property separates these measures, and it surprises experienced analysts. Take a trial with two equal strata, randomized within each, so there is no confounding.

Say the outcome is recovery from pneumonia within a week, with made-up numbers. Among older patients, recovery is fifty percent on placebo and eighty on treatment, an odds ratio of four.

Among younger patients it is twenty against fifty percent, again an odds ratio of exactly four.

Pool everyone, and recovery is thirty-five against sixty-five percent. The odds ratio is now three point four five.

So the pooled odds ratio is smaller than the odds ratio in every stratum, with no confounding at all.

That is non-collapsibility. The risk ratio and the risk difference pool to values between the strata, but the odds ratio and the hazard ratio do not.

So adding a prognostic covariate to a logistic or a Cox model moves the estimate away from one, even in a perfectly randomized trial.

That change is not confounding. The conditional and the marginal odds ratios simply answer different questions.

Put together, each measure answers its own question, and only some designs can deliver it.

A case-control study gives you the odds ratio alone, unless the baseline risk is known from elsewhere, while a trial or a cohort can give you every one of them.

One habit protects readers from most of these traps. Give the absolute risks, at a stated time, next to every ratio.

One last check, on a new cancer trial, drawn the way we drew the hazards.

In the control arm, eighty percent have died by ten years.

With a hazard ratio of 0.5 and proportional hazards, how many on the new drug have died by 10 years?

About fifty-five percent. Control survival is zero point two, and zero point two to the power of one half is zero point four five, so fifty-five percent have died: far from half of eighty.

On the curves, eighty percent dead in the control arm becomes about fifty-five on the new drug.

Not forty. Halving the hazard slows the deaths; it does not halve them.

So read every ratio as an answer to its own question, and ask for the absolute risks that say how many people it changes.
