Does a minimum wage cost jobs?

Why the textbook says a wage floor costs jobs, what Seattle and 138 state rises found, and why the answer depends on how high it goes.

Does a higher minimum wage help low-paid workers, or cost them their jobs? One side says a higher floor always destroys jobs.

The other says it never does. Both point to evidence.

Start with the textbook. Employers buy fewer hours of work as it gets dearer: demand for labour slopes down.

And more people want work, and longer hours, at a better wage.

Left alone, the market settles on a wage and a number of hours.

Now a law sets the lowest wage a little above that market wage.

At that wage more hours are offered than employers want to buy. So they buy fewer hours than before: the textbook's lost work.

Some evidence fits. When Seattle raised its minimum to thirteen dollars in 2016, a University of Washington team found low-wage hours fell about nine percent.

Some doesn't. Across 138 state minimum-wage rises, low-wage jobs barely changed in number: jobs below the new floor vanished, and as many appeared just above it.

The picture can fail where few employers compete for workers: they can pay below the market wage, and then a floor can raise pay without costing jobs.

Where the studies agree is size. Push the floor far above the local wage, and the gap becomes hard to argue away.

In 2019 the Congressional Budget Office judged that a fifteen-dollar federal minimum would raise pay for 17 million workers, and cost about 1.3 million jobs.

So neither claim survives as stated. The real question is not whether a floor costs jobs, but how high it can go before it does.

A $15 minimum comes to two places. Where is it most likely to cost jobs?

Where most jobs pay $10: there fifteen dollars is far above the market wage, so the gap between work offered and work wanted is widest.
