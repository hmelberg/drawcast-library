How a fridge keeps cold

Follow the coolant as it boils inside, is squeezed, and dumps your food's heat out the back: a fridge moves heat, it makes no cold.

How does a fridge stay cold in a warm kitchen? The surprise: it makes no cold at all — it carries heat out.

Inside it is 4 degrees, out here 20, and heat always creeps from warm to cold — so warmth leaks in through the walls all day.

So a pipe runs round the inside, carrying a coolant that boils at about minus 20 degrees.

Colder than the milk, it soaks up the milk's heat, and that heat makes it boil into gas.

Boiling swallows a lot of heat — the way drying sweat cools your skin. This is where the inside gets cold.

Now the heat is in the gas. A pump, the compressor, sucks that gas out of the cabinet and squeezes it hard.

Squeezed gas heats up: to about 45 degrees, hotter than the kitchen.

So in the coils on the back, heat flows out into the room, and the gas cools back into a liquid.

Last, the liquid squeezes through a narrow valve back into the cabinet. Its pressure drops, and at low pressure it boils at minus 20 again.

Round and round it goes: pick heat up inside, carry it out, dump it behind.

The whole trick is pressure: low where the coolant must be colder than the food,

high where it must be hotter than the room.

That squeeze is what the electricity pays for: pushing heat uphill, from cold to warm.

And it is why the back of a fridge feels warm: that is the heat that was in your food.

A quick check. On a hot day you leave the fridge door open. What happens to the kitchen?

It gets warmer: the fridge only moves heat from its inside to its back, both in the same room — and its motor adds heat of its own.
