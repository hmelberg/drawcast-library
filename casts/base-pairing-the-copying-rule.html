Why DNA pairs A with T and C with G

How the fit of big and small bases makes DNA its own template, so each strand can rebuild the other letter for letter

Why does DNA always pair A with T, and C with G? It comes down to fit, and that fit is what lets a cell copy itself. Start with one strand.

A second strand winds around it. Each is a backbone of sugar and phosphate: the two rails of a twisted ladder.

The rungs are bases, one from each strand, meeting in the middle. There are four kinds: A, T, C and G.

Unroll a short stretch of the ladder. Along the top strand it reads A, T, G, C, C, A.

Across from every A sits a T, across from every G a C, and never anything else.

Size, first. A and G are big, with two rings; T and C are small, with one. Big with small always spans the same width.

Two big ones would clash in the middle; two small ones would leave a gap. Either would bend the ladder.

And the partners lock: A holds T by two hydrogen bonds, G holds C by three.

Now unzip the ladder. Those bonds are weak, so the two strands come apart.

Take the bottom strand away, and its letters are gone.

But nothing is lost: the top strand dictates every partner, so the cell rebuilds the missing strand, letter for letter.

Watson and Crick saw this at once, and near the end of their 1953 paper they wrote, coyly:

'It has not escaped our notice that the specific pairing we have postulated immediately suggests a possible copying mechanism for the genetic material.'

So the pairing rule is a copying rule: each strand carries the other. That is how a dividing cell copies both sets of your three billion letters, six billion in all.

A sample of DNA is 30% A. What share is G?

Every A has a T, so T is thirty too, and the remaining forty splits evenly between G and C.
