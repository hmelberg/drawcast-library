Fermat's Last Theorem

Why two squares can make a square but two cubes never make a cube — and how a strange curve finally proved it, 358 years after Fermat's margin note.

Two squares can add up to a square. So why can two cubes never add up to a cube?

Like the three, four, five triangle?

Exactly. Three squared plus four squared is five squared: nine plus sixteen.

Are there many triples like that?

Infinitely many. Pick any two whole numbers, and an old recipe hands you a new triple.

Now raise the power to three, and the triangle is no help any more.

Surely, if I search long enough, I'll find two cubes that work.

Six cubed plus eight cubed: how much is that?

So close, and still no cube. In 1637 Pierre de Fermat claimed no power above two ever works.

He wrote it in the margin of an old Greek maths book, the Arithmetica of Diophantus.

And beside it: he had a truly marvellous proof, but the margin was too narrow?

Yes. Nobody ever found that proof, and the note cost mathematicians three hundred and fifty-eight years.

Here are the powers left to beat. Fermat himself knocked out four, with a trick called infinite descent.

Suppose a solution exists. From it he built a smaller one, and from that a smaller one again, forever.

But whole numbers can't shrink forever. You hit one, and stop.

Right, so there was never a first solution. And four takes eight, twelve, sixteen and twenty down with it.

Euler did three, in 1770, and that clears every multiple of three.

A tenth power is a fifth power of a square, so ten falls with five. Which others still need a proof? Tap them.

So only the odd primes are left.

Sophie Germain, around 1820, showed that for many primes, a solution would need that prime to divide a, b or c.

Then Kummer hit a wall. The proofs worked in bigger number systems, where numbers can split into primes in more than one way.

Six is two times three. But in one such system, it is also this.

Two different ways to split the same number? That breaks everything.

Kummer's repair covered every prime below a hundred except thirty-seven, fifty-nine and sixty-seven. Infinitely many still stood.

The way through came from somewhere else entirely: from curves like this one.

y squared equals x cubed minus x. That's an elliptic curve?

It is. Now forget the drawing. Count its whole-number solutions modulo a prime, in clock arithmetic.

Those counts make a fingerprint. Modular means some hugely symmetric function carries the very same fingerprint.

And in the 1950s Taniyama and Shimura guessed that every curve like this is modular?

Right. Then in 1984 Gerhard Frey had a wild idea: take a supposed solution, and build a curve from it.

Its curve crosses the axis at a to the p, and minus b to the p?

Yes, and with those huge numbers built in, it's so strange that Ken Ribet proved, in 1986, it cannot be modular.

So: a solution gives Frey's curve, and Ribet says Frey's curve is not modular.

But if every curve like that is modular…

…then Frey's curve is both at once, which is impossible.

And Andrew Wiles proved enough of that old guess to cover Frey's curve.

He announced it in Cambridge in 1993. A gap turned up; with Richard Taylor he closed it, and it was published in 1995.

So nobody ever checked all the numbers?

Nobody had to. Any solution would build a curve that must be modular and cannot be. So there is no solution.

Suppose Ribet's step had failed, and Frey's curve could be modular. Would Wiles's theorem alone prove Fermat?

No. Wiles shows the curve is modular; only Ribet's 'it cannot be' turns that into a contradiction.
