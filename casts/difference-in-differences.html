Measuring a policy against what never happened

How difference-in-differences borrows the world without a policy from a group it missed, and the parallel-trends bet it rests on.

How do you measure what a policy did, when you never see the world without it? Difference-in-differences borrows that world from a group the policy missed.

Say one region starts an apprenticeship scheme in 2020. Young workers' pay there goes from $18 an hour to $22 by 2025.

Before and after, the scheme seems worth $4 an hour.

But pay rose everywhere. In a similar region without the scheme it went from $16 to $17.50, with no scheme at all.

Before 2020 the two lines run parallel. The scheme region was $2 ahead, but the two moved in step.

The key assumption is that they would have kept moving in step: without the scheme, pay there would also have risen $1.50, to $19.50.

That dashed path is never observed. It is borrowed from the other region.

The effect is the gap between what happened and that path: $22 minus $19.50.

Two differences: the change with the scheme, minus the change without it. $2.50 an hour.

Not $4: before-and-after handed the scheme a rise that everyone got.

Subtracting changes, not levels, removes the $2 head start, which never moved, and the $1.50 rise both regions got anyway.

But suppose the scheme region had been pulling ahead anyway, and would have reached $20.50. Then the effect is only $1.50.

Nothing after 2020 can tell those two paths apart. Only the years before can make parallel trends believable.

John Snow used the idea in 1850s London: cholera deaths among customers of a water company that moved its intake upstream, against a company that did not.

So the groups need not be alike. They need to be groups that would have changed alike.

Before the policy, pay in the treated region was $5 higher than in the control. Does that break difference-in-differences?

No. A steady gap is subtracted away with the first differences; what breaks the method is a gap that would have changed without the policy.
