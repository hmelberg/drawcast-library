How much can a one percent yearly fee cost? Follow a hundred dollars growing at seven percent a year for fifty years.

Without a fee, the whole pot earns seven percent each year, and it roughly doubles every decade.

After fifty years: two thousand nine hundred and forty-six dollars.

Now a fund keeps one percent of the savings every year. The fee is this slider.

Each year the pot grows seven percent, then gives up one percent of itself. Same money, same markets.

After fifty years: one thousand seven hundred and eighty-two.

So a fee of one percent a year took about two fifths of the final pot.

The fee compounds too: every dollar it takes early stops earning for all the years that follow.

Slide the fee up to two percent, and the last bar sinks to just over a third of the fee-free pot.

One thousand and seventy-three dollars.

This is why John Bogle started the first index fund for ordinary savers in 1976: to keep fees close to nothing.
