A 99 % accurate test says yes. Are you ill?

Why a positive on a 99 % accurate test can be a coin flip when the disease is rare, and why a second test settles it

Does a positive on a ninety-nine percent accurate test mean you are probably ill? First, what that one number hides. Here are a hundred people like you.

Say one person in a hundred has the disease. Just this one.

A test that tells everyone they are healthy is right about ninety-nine of these hundred. Ninety-nine percent accurate, and useless.

So accuracy alone says little. A test has two numbers: how often it catches the sick, and how often it wrongly flags the healthy.

A good test catches ninety-nine percent of the sick, so this person tests positive.

It also flags one healthy person in a hundred. Among ninety-nine healthy people that is about one: this person, perfectly well.

Two positive results, and only one of them is ill. Your positive is a coin flip.

Weigh the two ways to get a positive: one sick person, caught, against ninety-nine healthy people, times a one percent slip.

When the false-alarm rate is as large as the share who are ill, a positive is even odds, however good the test sounds.

Now retest just these two, with a second test whose mistakes are independent. In this new crowd, one in two is ill.

The healthy crowd has shrunk from ninety-nine to one. A second positive means ninety-nine to one: ninety-nine percent.

The first positive didn't settle it. It picked out a smaller crowd, in which the same kind of test is finally convincing.

Thomas Bayes worked out this kind of updating; his essay was published in 1763, two years after his death, by his friend Richard Price.
