Corners minus edges plus faces: what does a cube give?

Count a cube's corners, edges and faces, type what V − E + F gives, and see why Euler's answer, 2, holds for every solid without holes.

Take a cube. Count its corners, take away its edges, and add its faces. What do you get?

For a cube: corners minus edges plus faces. Type the number you get.

Two: eight corners, minus twelve edges, plus six faces.

Eight corners, twelve edges,

and six faces: the front, the back, and four around the sides.

So eight minus twelve plus six makes two.

Now a pyramid with a square base: five corners, eight edges, five faces.

Two again. And it works for every solid without holes, however many sides it has.

A football's panels: sixty corners, ninety edges, thirty-two faces. Two.

Leonhard Euler spotted the rule in 1750, and wrote about it to his friend Christian Goldbach.

But drill a hole through a solid, like this picture frame,

and the answer drops to zero.

So the number keeps track of holes, and that idea grew into a whole field of maths: topology.
